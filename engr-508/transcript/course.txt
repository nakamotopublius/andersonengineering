0.3 Linear algebra is the mathematics of vectors and matrices: of quantities that come in lists, and of operations that combine them in proportion. It starts with systems of linear equations, many unknowns related by many equations, and grows into a way of thinking about high-dimensional spaces. Where calculus studies change, linear algebra studies structure, and in modern engineering it is used at least as much as calculus.
0.4 Nearly every large computation in engineering ends in linear algebra. A structural analysis of a bridge solves a system with thousands or millions of unknown displacements. A circuit simulator solves for node voltages. Computer graphics rotates and projects shapes with matrices. Control systems, signal processing, statistics, machine learning, and search engines are all built on it. Behind every one of these sits a matrix, and the questions of this course: can we solve it, how, and what do its eigenvalues say?
0.5 Here is the map of this course, which follows MIT's 18.06, Linear Algebra. Lecture one introduces vectors, linear combinations, and the equation A x equals b, seen through rows and through columns. Lecture two solves it by elimination and describes matrix multiplication, inverses, and the factorisation A equals L U. Lecture three introduces vector spaces, independence, rank, and the four fundamental subspaces of a matrix.
0.6 Lecture four turns to orthogonality: projections, least-squares fitting of data, and orthonormal bases. Lecture five introduces determinants and then eigenvalues and eigenvectors, and uses them for powers of matrices, Markov chains, and differential equations. Lecture six treats symmetric and positive definite matrices and closes with the singular value decomposition, which many regard as the summit of the subject, with applications from data analysis to image compression.

If you have taken Engineering 507 B, Differential Equations, you have already met eigenvalues; this course explains them properly. @L 1 | Vectors, Linear Combinations, and A x = b | Vectors and their operations, linear combinations, the dot product, matrices, and the row and column pictures of a linear system.
1.1 A vector is an ordered list of numbers. The vector with components two and three can be drawn as an arrow from the origin to the point two, three in the plane. In three dimensions a vector has three components, and nothing stops us from using vectors with ten components, or a million, even though we cannot draw them. A vector with n components lives in the space called R n, the space of all such lists of real numbers.
1.2 Two operations define everything that follows. Vectors add component by component: one, two plus three, four equals four, six. And a vector is multiplied by a number, called a scalar, component by component: three times one, two equals three, six. Combining these gives a linear combination: c times v plus d times w, for numbers c and d. Linear combinations are the central idea of the whole subject.
1.3 Picture all the linear combinations of a single non-zero vector v in three dimensions: they fill a line through the origin. All combinations of two vectors v and w that do not lie on the same line fill a plane through the origin. All combinations of three vectors that do not lie in a common plane fill all of three-dimensional space. Much of linear algebra asks, for a given set of vectors, which of these happens.
1.4 The dot product of two vectors multiplies matching components and adds: for one, two and three, four it is one times three plus two times four, which is eleven. The length of a vector is the square root of its dot product with itself, so the vector three, four has length five. The dot product also measures angle: v dot w equals the length of v times the length of w times the cosine of the angle between them. Two vectors are perpendicular, or orthogonal, exactly when their dot product is zero.
1.5 A matrix is a rectangular array of numbers, with m rows and n columns. Multiplying a matrix A by a vector x gives a new vector, A x, and there are two ways to compute it. The row way: each component of A x is the dot product of a row of A with x. The column way: A x is a linear combination of the columns of A, using the components of x as the weights. Both give the same answer, and Gilbert Strang emphasises that the column way is the deeper one.
1.6 A system of linear equations can be written as A x equals b. Take the system two x minus y equals zero, and minus x plus two y equals three. The matrix A has rows two, minus one and minus one, two; the unknown vector x has components x and y; and the right side b has components zero and three. Solving the system means finding the x that A transforms into b.
1.7 The row picture views each equation as a line in the plane. The first equation, two x minus y equals zero, is the line y equals two x. The second, minus x plus two y equals three, is another line. The solution is the point where they meet, here x equals one and y equals two. Check: two times one minus two is zero, and minus one plus four is three. In three dimensions, each equation is a plane, and the solution is the point common to three planes.
1.8 The column picture views the same system as a question about combinations. The columns of A are two, minus one and minus one, two. We want numbers x and y so that x times the first column plus y times the second column equals the vector zero, three. The answer is one of the first column plus two of the second: two, minus one plus minus two, four equals zero, three. The same numbers solve both pictures, but the column picture generalises better to higher dimensions.
1.9 When does A x equals b have a solution for every b? In the column picture, exactly when the combinations of the columns fill the whole space. For a square matrix with n columns in n dimensions, that happens when the columns point in genuinely different directions, so that none is a combination of the others. Such a matrix is called invertible, or non-singular. If the columns all lie in a lower-dimensional plane or line, most right-hand sides b cannot be reached.
1.10 A singular example makes this concrete. If the columns of a two-by-two matrix are one, two and two, four, the second column is twice the first, so all combinations lie on one line. The equation A x equals b can be solved only when b lies on that line, and then it has infinitely many solutions. In the row picture, the two equations describe parallel lines, which never meet, or the same line, which meet everywhere. Singular systems have either no solution or infinitely many.
1.11 Matrices are also transformations. A matrix A takes each input vector x to an output vector A x, and it does so linearly: A of x plus y equals A x plus A y, and A of c x equals c times A x. A two-by-two matrix can rotate the plane, stretch it, reflect it, or shear it. The rotation through an angle theta has rows cos theta, minus sin theta and sin theta, cos theta. Computer graphics applies such matrices millions of times a second.
1.12 Engineering systems lead naturally to A x equals b. In a truss, the unknowns are the forces in the members, and each joint gives equilibrium equations. In an electrical network, the unknowns are node voltages, and Kirchhoff's current law gives one equation per node. In a chemical reaction, balancing atoms of each element gives linear equations for the coefficients. Modern finite-element models of structures and fluids can have millions of unknowns.
1.13 There are important special vectors and matrices. The zero vector has all components zero. A unit vector has length one; dividing any non-zero vector by its length makes a unit vector in the same direction. The identity matrix I has ones on its main diagonal and zeros elsewhere, and I x equals x for every x. A diagonal matrix has non-zero entries only on its diagonal and simply scales each component.
1.14 Linear algebra has a geometric soul and a computational body. The geometry, combinations, spaces, angles, and lengths, tells us what questions to ask and what the answers mean. The computation, the systematic arithmetic of the next lecture, produces actual numbers. Engineers need both: the geometry to understand a model, and the computation to run it at scale on a computer.
1.15 The notation of this subject is compact, and it rewards reading slowly. Capital letters usually mean matrices; lower-case bold or plain letters mean vectors; Greek letters often mean scalars. The expression A x equals b packs an entire system into four symbols. Whenever you meet a matrix expression, ask what shape each object has, m by n, n by one, and check that the shapes fit together.
1.16 To summarise: vectors add and scale, and their linear combinations fill lines, planes, and whole spaces. The dot product gives length and angle. A matrix times a vector is a combination of its columns. A x equals b has a row picture, intersecting lines or planes, and a column picture, combining columns to reach b. Invertible matrices give exactly one solution; singular ones give none or infinitely many. Next we solve A x equals b systematically.

@L 2 | Elimination, Matrix Multiplication, and Inverses | Gaussian elimination and pivots, back substitution, matrix multiplication, elimination matrices, the factorisation A = LU, inverses, and transposes.
2.1 The standard way to solve A x equals b is elimination, often called Gaussian elimination after Carl Friedrich Gauss, though the method appears in a Chinese text, The Nine Chapters on the Mathematical Art, nearly two thousand years earlier. The idea is to subtract multiples of one equation from others to remove unknowns, step by step, until the system becomes triangular and can be solved from the bottom up.
2.2 Take the system x plus two y equals three, and three x plus eight y equals five. Subtract three times the first equation from the second: the x disappears, leaving two y equals minus four. The number three is called the multiplier, and the coefficient one, of x in the first equation, is the pivot. Now the system is upper triangular. Back substitution solves the last equation first, y equals minus two, and then the first, x equals three minus two y, which is seven.
2.3 For a larger system, elimination works column by column. Use the first equation's first coefficient as a pivot to clear the first column below it. Then use the new second equation's second coefficient as the next pivot to clear the second column below it, and so on. The result is an upper triangular matrix U, with the pivots on its diagonal. For an n by n matrix, elimination needs about n cubed over three multiplications, which is why doubling the size of a model makes it about eight times slower to solve.
2.4 Elimination can break down. If a zero appears in a pivot position, exchange that equation with a lower one that has a non-zero entry there, a row exchange. If no lower equation helps, the matrix is singular, and the system has no solution or infinitely many. On a computer, programs exchange rows even when the pivot is merely small, a strategy called partial pivoting, because dividing by tiny pivots magnifies rounding errors.
2.5 Matrix multiplication describes combining transformations. The product A B is defined so that A B times x equals A times B x: first apply B, then A. The entry in row i and column j of A B is the dot product of row i of A with column j of B. A B makes sense only when the number of columns of A equals the number of rows of B. Multiplication is associative, A B times C equals A times B C, but generally not commutative: A B and B A are usually different.
2.6 Each step of elimination is itself a matrix multiplication. Subtracting three times row one from row two is the same as multiplying by an elimination matrix E, which is the identity matrix with minus three placed in row two, column one. A row exchange is multiplication by a permutation matrix P, the identity with two rows swapped. Writing elimination as matrices lets us record the whole process algebraically.
2.7 Recording the multipliers gives the most important factorisation in computational linear algebra: A equals L U. Here U is the upper triangular result of elimination, and L is lower triangular, with ones on its diagonal and the multipliers below it, each in the position where it was used. When row exchanges are needed, the factorisation is P A equals L U. Once L and U are known, every new right-hand side b is solved quickly by two triangular solves, forward with L and backward with U.
2.8 That is why engineering software factors a matrix once and reuses the factors. A structural model may be analysed under hundreds of load cases, each a different right-hand side b, with the same stiffness matrix A. The expensive factorisation, proportional to n cubed, is done once; each new load case then costs only about n squared operations.
2.9 A square matrix A is invertible if there is a matrix A inverse such that A inverse times A equals the identity, and A times A inverse equals the identity too. Then A x equals b has the unique solution x equals A inverse b. A matrix is invertible exactly when elimination produces n non-zero pivots. For a two-by-two matrix with rows a, b and c, d, the inverse is one over a d minus b c times the matrix with rows d, minus b and minus c, a, provided a d minus b c is not zero.
2.10 Inverses obey a reversal rule: the inverse of A B is B inverse times A inverse. To undo putting on socks and then shoes, take off the shoes first. The inverse can be computed by the Gauss-Jordan method, which applies elimination to A alongside the identity matrix until A becomes the identity, at which point the identity has become A inverse. In practice, engineers rarely compute inverses explicitly; solving with L and U is faster and more accurate.
2.11 The transpose of a matrix, written A transpose, swaps its rows and columns: the entry in row i, column j becomes the entry in row j, column i. The transpose of a product reverses the order: A B transpose equals B transpose A transpose. The dot product of x and y can be written x transpose y, a row times a column. These small facts are used constantly in the rest of the course.
2.12 A matrix equal to its own transpose is symmetric. Symmetric matrices arise naturally in engineering: stiffness matrices of structures, conductance matrices of circuits, and covariance matrices of data are all symmetric. For any matrix R, the product R transpose R is square and symmetric, a combination we will meet in least squares. Symmetric matrices have special and beautiful properties, the subject of lecture six.
2.13 Most large engineering matrices are sparse: almost all of their entries are zero, because each unknown interacts only with a few neighbours. A finite-element model with a million unknowns may have only a few dozen non-zeros per row. Sparse elimination methods order the unknowns cleverly to keep the factors sparse, and iterative methods improve an approximate solution step by step without factoring at all. Both are essential for very large problems.
2.14 Round-off error matters because computers store numbers with finite precision, about sixteen significant digits in standard double precision. Some matrices are ill-conditioned: tiny changes in b or A cause large changes in x. The condition number measures this sensitivity; a condition number of ten to the k means that roughly k digits of accuracy may be lost. An engineer who sees a large condition number should suspect the model, not just the arithmetic.
2.15 Block matrices extend all of this. A large matrix can be divided into smaller blocks, and the blocks multiply following the same rules as entries, as long as the shapes fit. Block thinking is how engineers organise large systems with distinct parts, such as a structure made of several substructures, and how computers organise multiplication to use memory efficiently.
2.16 To summarise: elimination reduces A x equals b to a triangular system, solved by back substitution. Pivots must be non-zero, with row exchanges when needed. Elimination steps are matrix multiplications, and recording them gives A equals L U, or P A equals L U. An invertible matrix has n pivots and an inverse, with the inverse of a product reversing the order. Transposes swap rows and columns, and symmetric matrices equal their transposes. Next we step back to see the structure of all solutions.

@L 3 | Vector Spaces, Independence, and the Four Subspaces | Vector spaces and subspaces, the column space and nullspace, the complete solution of A x = b, independence, basis, dimension, rank, and the four fundamental subspaces.
3.1 A vector space is a collection of vectors closed under addition and scalar multiplication: adding any two vectors in it, or multiplying one by any number, gives another vector still in it. R n is a vector space. A subspace is a vector space inside a larger one. In three dimensions, the subspaces are the origin alone, every line through the origin, every plane through the origin, and the whole space. A line or plane that misses the origin is not a subspace, because zero times any vector is the zero vector.
3.2 The column space of a matrix A, written C of A, is the set of all linear combinations of its columns. It is a subspace, and it answers a basic question: A x equals b has a solution exactly when b is in the column space of A. For a three-by-two matrix with independent columns, the column space is a plane in three-dimensional space, and only right-hand sides lying in that plane can be reached.
3.3 The nullspace of A, written N of A, is the set of all solutions of A x equals zero. It is also a subspace: if A x and A y are both zero, so is A of x plus y. The nullspace always contains the zero vector. If it contains anything else, then A x equals b, when it has a solution, has infinitely many, because we can add any nullspace vector to a solution and still have a solution.
3.4 To find the nullspace, apply elimination to reach a staircase form, called the row echelon form, or, going further, the reduced row echelon form. Columns with pivots correspond to pivot variables; the remaining columns correspond to free variables, which may be chosen freely. Setting each free variable to one in turn, and the others to zero, and solving for the pivot variables gives special solutions. Every vector in the nullspace is a combination of the special solutions.
3.5 The complete solution of A x equals b, when a solution exists, has two parts: x equals a particular solution plus any vector in the nullspace. The particular solution solves A x equals b; the nullspace part solves A x equals zero and adds nothing. This is the same structure as for linear differential equations in Engineering 507 B, where the general solution was a particular solution plus the homogeneous solution. Linearity produces it in both places.
3.6 Vectors are linearly independent if no one of them is a combination of the others; equivalently, the only combination that gives the zero vector is the one with all weights zero. Independent vectors point in genuinely new directions. The columns of A are independent exactly when the nullspace of A contains only the zero vector. Any set of more than n vectors in R n must be dependent: four vectors in three-dimensional space cannot all be independent.
3.7 A basis for a space is a set of vectors that is independent and spans the space, meaning their combinations fill it. Every vector in the space can then be written as a combination of the basis vectors in exactly one way. A space has many bases, but every basis has the same number of vectors, called the dimension of the space. The standard basis of R three is the three unit vectors along the axes, but any three independent vectors also form a basis.
3.8 The rank of a matrix, written r, is the number of pivots that elimination produces. It equals the number of independent columns, the dimension of the column space, and also the number of independent rows. The rank is the true size of a matrix: a one-hundred-by-one-hundred matrix of rank three carries only as much information as three columns. A matrix of rank one is a column times a row, the simplest building block of all.
3.9 The rank determines how many solutions a system has. For an m by n matrix of rank r, there are r pivot variables and n minus r free variables, so the nullspace has dimension n minus r. If r equals n, the columns are independent and there is at most one solution. If r equals m, the column space is all of R m and there is at least one solution for every b. If r equals m equals n, the matrix is square and invertible, with exactly one solution.
3.10 Every m by n matrix has four fundamental subspaces, a picture that Gilbert Strang places at the centre of 18.06. Two are subspaces of R n: the row space, which is the column space of A transpose, with dimension r, and the nullspace, with dimension n minus r. Two are subspaces of R m: the column space, with dimension r, and the left nullspace, the nullspace of A transpose, with dimension m minus r.
3.11 The dimensions add up neatly: in R n, the row space and nullspace have dimensions r and n minus r, totalling n; in R m, the column space and left nullspace have dimensions r and m minus r, totalling m. This is the fundamental theorem of linear algebra, part one. Part two, the subject of the next lecture, says that each pair of subspaces is perpendicular: every vector in the nullspace is orthogonal to every vector in the row space.
3.12 The left nullspace has a clear meaning in applications. In an electrical network, A can be the incidence matrix recording which nodes each wire connects. Its nullspace describes potentials that produce no current, constant across a connected network, and its left nullspace describes currents that flow around loops, satisfying Kirchhoff's current law. The four subspaces of this one matrix encode the basic laws of circuits.
3.13 Vector spaces need not consist of lists of numbers. The set of all polynomials of degree at most three is a vector space of dimension four, with basis one, x, x squared, x cubed. The solutions of a linear homogeneous differential equation of order n form a vector space of dimension n, which is why Engineering 507 B needed exactly two independent solutions for a second-order equation. Functions, signals, and images can all be treated as vectors.
3.14 Change of basis is a powerful idea. The same vector has different components in different bases, and a good basis can make a problem simple. Fourier series, from Engineering 507 B, express a signal in a basis of sines and cosines; the JPEG image format uses a related cosine basis, and wavelets provide another. Choosing a basis in which a matrix becomes diagonal is the goal of eigenvalues, in lecture five.
3.15 Linear transformations between spaces are represented by matrices once bases are chosen. Differentiation, for example, is a linear transformation on polynomials: it takes a plus b x plus c x squared to b plus two c x. In the basis one, x, x squared, it is represented by a three-by-three matrix. This viewpoint unifies calculus and algebra: many operations that look different are linear transformations in disguise.
3.16 To summarise: vector spaces and subspaces are closed under combinations. The column space contains every reachable b; the nullspace contains every solution of A x equals zero. The complete solution is particular plus nullspace. Independence, basis, and dimension measure the true size of a space, and the rank measures the true size of a matrix. The four fundamental subspaces have dimensions r, n minus r, r, and m minus r. Next we bring in perpendicularity.

@L 4 | Orthogonality, Projections, and Least Squares | Orthogonal subspaces, projection onto a line and onto a subspace, least-squares fitting, the normal equations, orthonormal bases, Gram-Schmidt, and A = QR.
4.1 Two vectors are orthogonal when their dot product is zero. Two subspaces are orthogonal when every vector in one is orthogonal to every vector in the other. The row space and nullspace of a matrix are orthogonal: if A x equals zero, then x has zero dot product with every row of A, and hence with every combination of rows. Likewise the column space and left nullspace are orthogonal. Moreover, each pair fills its whole space, so they are orthogonal complements.
4.2 Projection answers a basic question: what is the closest point to a vector b on a given line or plane? Projecting b onto the line through a vector a gives p equals a times the fraction a transpose b over a transpose a. The error e equals b minus p is perpendicular to a; that perpendicularity is what makes p the closest point. For example, projecting b equals one, one, one onto a equals one, zero, zero gives p equals one, zero, zero, with error zero, one, one.
4.3 Projecting onto a subspace spanned by the independent columns of a matrix A is similar. We seek the combination A x hat closest to b. The error b minus A x hat must be perpendicular to every column of A, which means A transpose times b minus A x hat equals zero. Rearranging gives the normal equations: A transpose A x hat equals A transpose b. The projection is p equals A x hat, and the projection matrix is P equals A times A transpose A inverse times A transpose.
4.4 Projection matrices have two characteristic properties: P is symmetric, and P squared equals P, because projecting a second time changes nothing. The matrix I minus P projects onto the perpendicular complement. Every vector b splits into two perpendicular pieces, P b and b minus P b, one in the subspace and one orthogonal to it, and by Pythagoras their squared lengths add to the squared length of b.
4.5 The most common use of projection is least squares. When there are more equations than unknowns, as with many measurements and few parameters, A x equals b usually has no exact solution, because b is not in the column space. The least-squares solution x hat minimises the sum of the squared errors, the squared length of b minus A x. It is found exactly by the normal equations: A transpose A x hat equals A transpose b. The idea goes back to Adrien-Marie Legendre in 1805 and Gauss, who used it to predict the orbit of the asteroid Ceres.
4.6 Fitting a straight line to data is the standard example. Suppose measurements at times t equals zero, one, and two give b equals six, zero, and zero. We seek C plus D t that fits best. The matrix A has rows one, zero; one, one; one, two. Solving the normal equations gives C equals five and D equals minus three, so the best line is five minus three t. Its errors at the three times are one, minus two, and one; they are perpendicular to both columns of A, as the theory requires.
4.7 Least squares is everywhere in engineering. Calibrating a sensor fits a line or curve to measured data. Surveyors adjust networks of measured angles and distances by least squares. Global positioning systems estimate position from more satellite signals than unknowns. In statistics, the same equations give linear regression, as Engineering 509 B will show. The method fits any model that is linear in its unknown parameters, including polynomials and sums of sines.
4.8 Calculations become much simpler with an orthonormal basis: vectors that are all unit length and mutually perpendicular. Put such vectors as the columns of a matrix Q; then Q transpose Q equals I. If Q is square, it is called an orthogonal matrix, and its inverse is simply its transpose. Rotations and reflections are orthogonal matrices. Multiplying by Q preserves lengths and angles, which makes orthogonal matrices numerically stable: they never magnify errors.
4.9 With orthonormal columns, the normal equations collapse. Q transpose Q is the identity, so the least-squares solution is simply x hat equals Q transpose b, and the projection is Q Q transpose b. Each component is a single dot product, with no system to solve. This is exactly how Fourier coefficients are computed, because sines and cosines of different frequencies form an orthogonal basis for functions.
4.10 The Gram-Schmidt process turns any independent set of vectors into an orthonormal set spanning the same space. Take the first vector and divide by its length. From the second vector subtract its projection onto the first, then divide by the length of what remains. From the third, subtract its projections onto the first two, then normalise, and so on. Each new vector is the part of the original that is perpendicular to everything before.
4.11 Recording the Gram-Schmidt steps gives a factorisation: A equals Q R, where Q has orthonormal columns and R is upper triangular. Then the least-squares problem becomes R x hat equals Q transpose b, a triangular system solved by back substitution. Engineering software prefers this route to the normal equations because forming A transpose A squares the condition number, losing accuracy, while Q R does not. In practice, Q R is computed by more stable variants, such as Householder reflections.
4.12 Orthogonality gives a simple test of whether data fit a model well. The least-squares error vector is orthogonal to the column space, so the squared length of b equals the squared length of the fitted part plus the squared length of the error. The fraction explained by the fit is the quantity statisticians call R squared. A good fit leaves an error that looks like random noise, with no pattern left to explain.
4.13 Least squares has pitfalls. If the columns of A are nearly dependent, the fit is ill-conditioned and the coefficients can swing wildly with small changes in the data. A single wild measurement, an outlier, can drag the line far from the rest, because errors are squared. And a model with too many parameters can fit the data perfectly while predicting new data badly, a problem called overfitting. Engineers guard against these with good experimental design and validation data.
4.14 The geometry of orthogonality extends to infinite-dimensional spaces of functions, where the dot product becomes an integral of a product. Fourier series are projections onto sines and cosines. Legendre polynomials are an orthogonal basis for functions on an interval, produced by Gram-Schmidt from one, x, x squared, and so on. The finite element method seeks the best approximation to a solution in a space of simple functions, which is again a projection.
4.15 Orthogonality also explains why some coordinate systems are pleasant and others are not. In a rectangular, or Cartesian, system the axes are orthonormal, so components are found by dot products and Pythagoras applies. In a skewed system, the components interact. Much of the art of engineering analysis is choosing perpendicular coordinates matched to the problem, so that the pieces separate and can be handled one at a time.
4.16 To summarise: the row space and nullspace, and the column space and left nullspace, are orthogonal complements. Projection finds the closest point in a subspace; the error is perpendicular. When A x equals b has no solution, least squares solves the normal equations A transpose A x hat equals A transpose b, fitting lines and models to data. Orthonormal bases make projections simple, Gram-Schmidt builds them, and A equals Q R records the process. Next we meet determinants and eigenvalues.

@L 5 | Determinants and Eigenvalues | Properties of determinants, their geometric meaning, eigenvalues and eigenvectors, the characteristic equation, diagonalisation, powers of matrices, Markov chains, and differential equations.
5.1 The determinant is a single number computed from a square matrix that tells whether the matrix is invertible: the determinant is non-zero exactly when the matrix is invertible. For a two-by-two matrix with rows a, b and c, d, the determinant is a d minus b c. Geometrically, its absolute value is the area of the parallelogram formed by the columns, and in three dimensions it is the volume of the box formed by the three columns. A zero determinant means the box is flat.
5.2 Determinants obey simple rules. The determinant of the identity is one. Exchanging two rows reverses the sign. The determinant is linear in each row separately. From these follow the rest: subtracting a multiple of one row from another leaves the determinant unchanged, a row of zeros makes it zero, and the determinant of a triangular matrix is the product of its diagonal entries. So the determinant equals plus or minus the product of the pivots from elimination, which is how computers calculate it.
5.3 Two further properties are especially useful. The determinant of a product is the product of the determinants: det of A B equals det A times det B. And the determinant of the transpose equals the determinant of the matrix. It follows that the determinant of A inverse is one over the determinant of A. Cramer's rule expresses each unknown of A x equals b as a ratio of determinants; it is elegant for small systems but far too slow for large ones.
5.4 Now the central idea of the second half of linear algebra. Most vectors change direction when multiplied by a matrix A. Special vectors, called eigenvectors, do not: A x equals lambda x, so A merely stretches or shrinks x by a number lambda, the eigenvalue. The word eigen is German for own or characteristic. Eigenvectors are the natural directions of a matrix, and eigenvalues say what the matrix does along them.
5.5 To find eigenvalues, rewrite A x equals lambda x as A minus lambda I, times x, equals zero. For a non-zero solution x to exist, A minus lambda I must be singular, so its determinant must be zero. This determinant is a polynomial of degree n in lambda, the characteristic polynomial, and its roots are the eigenvalues. For each eigenvalue, the eigenvectors are the non-zero vectors in the nullspace of A minus lambda I.
5.6 As an example, take the symmetric matrix with rows two, one and one, two. The determinant of A minus lambda I is two minus lambda, squared, minus one, which equals lambda squared minus four lambda plus three, with roots lambda equals three and lambda equals one. For lambda equals three, the eigenvector is one, one: A times one, one is three, three. For lambda equals one, it is one, minus one: A times one, minus one is one, minus one.
5.7 Two quick checks catch most errors. The sum of the eigenvalues equals the trace, the sum of the diagonal entries: here three plus one equals two plus two. The product of the eigenvalues equals the determinant: three times one equals four minus one. Eigenvalues can be complex even for a real matrix: the rotation through ninety degrees turns every real vector, so it has no real eigenvectors, and its eigenvalues are i and minus i.
5.8 If an n by n matrix has n independent eigenvectors, put them as the columns of a matrix X, and put the eigenvalues on the diagonal of a matrix capital Lambda. Then A X equals X Lambda, so A equals X Lambda X inverse. This is diagonalisation: in the basis of eigenvectors, A acts by simply stretching each coordinate. A matrix with n distinct eigenvalues is always diagonalisable. Some matrices with repeated eigenvalues lack enough eigenvectors and cannot be diagonalised.
5.9 Diagonalisation makes powers of a matrix easy. A squared equals X Lambda X inverse X Lambda X inverse, which equals X Lambda squared X inverse, and in general A to the k equals X Lambda to the k X inverse. Powers of a diagonal matrix just raise each diagonal entry to the power k. So the long-run behaviour of A to the k is controlled by the eigenvalue of largest absolute value: if every eigenvalue is smaller than one in size, A to the k goes to zero.
5.10 The Fibonacci numbers, zero, one, one, two, three, five, eight, and so on, give a neat example. Each number is the sum of the previous two, which can be written as a two-by-two matrix acting on a pair of consecutive numbers. The eigenvalues of that matrix are one plus the square root of five, over two, about one point six one eight, the golden ratio, and one minus the square root of five, over two, about minus zero point six one eight. So the Fibonacci numbers grow by a factor approaching the golden ratio at each step.
5.11 A Markov matrix has non-negative entries and columns that each add to one. It describes probabilities of moving between states, such as the fraction of a population moving between cities each year. Every Markov matrix has an eigenvalue equal to one, and, when all its entries are positive, every other eigenvalue is smaller than one in size. So repeated application approaches a steady state: the eigenvector for eigenvalue one, which gives the long-run fraction in each state.
5.12 Google's original PageRank algorithm, described by Sergey Brin and Larry Page in 1998, ranks web pages using exactly this idea. It imagines a surfer following links at random, occasionally jumping to a random page, and computes the steady-state probability of being on each page: the eigenvector for eigenvalue one of an enormous Markov matrix. Pages that are linked from many important pages rank highly.
5.13 Eigenvalues also solve systems of differential equations, as Engineering 507 B showed. For d u by d t equals A u, each eigenvector x with eigenvalue lambda gives a solution e to the lambda t times x, and the general solution is a combination of these. The system is stable, with all solutions decaying, when every eigenvalue has a negative real part. In matrix form, the solution is u of t equals e to the A t times u of zero, where the matrix exponential equals X e to the Lambda t X inverse.
5.14 In engineering, eigenvalues often have direct physical meaning. The eigenvalues of a structure's stiffness and mass matrices give the squares of its natural frequencies, and the eigenvectors give its mode shapes. The eigenvalues of a control system's matrix show whether it is stable. In buckling analysis, the smallest eigenvalue gives the critical load at which a column bends sideways. In quantum mechanics, eigenvalues are the allowed energies of an atom.
5.15 Computing eigenvalues of large matrices is never done by finding roots of the characteristic polynomial, which is numerically hopeless for large n. Instead, computers use iterative methods. The simplest, the power method, multiplies a vector by A over and over, which brings out the eigenvector of the largest eigenvalue. The Q R algorithm, developed around 1961 by John Francis and Vera Kublanovskaya, finds all eigenvalues and is one of the most important algorithms of the twentieth century.
5.16 To summarise: the determinant is zero exactly for singular matrices; it measures area and volume, multiplies for products, and equals the product of the pivots up to sign. Eigenvectors satisfy A x equals lambda x, and eigenvalues are roots of det of A minus lambda I. Their sum is the trace and their product the determinant. Diagonalisation, A equals X Lambda X inverse, gives powers of A, Markov steady states, and solutions of differential equations. Next we meet the most important matrices of all, symmetric ones.

@L 6 | Symmetric Matrices and the Singular Value Decomposition | The spectral theorem, positive definite matrices and energy, the singular value decomposition, low-rank approximation, principal component analysis, and linear algebra in engineering practice.
6.1 Symmetric matrices, equal to their own transposes, have the most beautiful eigenvalue theory. The spectral theorem states that a real symmetric matrix has only real eigenvalues, and that its eigenvectors can be chosen to be orthonormal. So every symmetric matrix can be written as S equals Q Lambda Q transpose, where Q is an orthogonal matrix of eigenvectors and Lambda is diagonal. The matrix with rows two, one and one, two from lecture five is an example: its eigenvectors one, one and one, minus one are perpendicular.
6.2 The spectral theorem has a vivid meaning. Q Lambda Q transpose says that a symmetric matrix acts by rotating to its eigenvector axes, stretching along each axis by its eigenvalue, and rotating back. Equivalently, S is a sum of simple pieces: each eigenvalue times its unit eigenvector times that eigenvector's transpose, each piece a projection onto one axis. Engineers call the eigenvector axes principal axes, as in the principal stresses of a loaded material.
6.3 A symmetric matrix is positive definite if all its eigenvalues are positive. There are several equivalent tests: all pivots are positive; all the upper-left determinants are positive; or, most important, the energy x transpose S x is positive for every non-zero vector x. For the two-by-two matrix with rows a, b and b, c, positive definiteness means a is positive and a c minus b squared is positive. The matrix with rows two, one and one, two passes, with eigenvalues three and one.
6.4 Positive definite matrices are the matrices of energy and stability. The stiffness matrix of a properly supported structure is positive definite, because deforming it in any way stores positive strain energy. A function's matrix of second derivatives, its Hessian, being positive definite at a critical point means the point is a minimum, which is the second-derivative test of Engineering 502 B in matrix form. Covariance matrices in statistics are positive semidefinite, with eigenvalues greater than or equal to zero.
6.5 Positive definite matrices also make computation easier. They need no row exchanges in elimination, and they factor as S equals L L transpose, the Cholesky factorisation, about twice as fast as general L U. The surface x transpose S x equals one is an ellipse in two dimensions, or an ellipsoid in more, with axes along the eigenvectors and half-lengths one over the square root of each eigenvalue.
6.6 Most matrices are not symmetric, and many are not even square. For every matrix, though, there is a factorisation as good as the spectral theorem: the singular value decomposition, or SVD. Any m by n matrix A can be written as A equals U Sigma V transpose, where U and V are orthogonal matrices and Sigma is a diagonal matrix of non-negative numbers called the singular values, arranged from largest to smallest. The number of non-zero singular values is the rank.
6.7 The SVD says that every matrix, in the right orthonormal bases, is diagonal. The columns of V, the right singular vectors, are a special orthonormal basis for the input space, and the columns of U, the left singular vectors, are a special basis for the output space, such that A times v i equals sigma i times u i. Geometrically, every matrix rotates, stretches along perpendicular axes by the singular values, and rotates again. A circle is mapped to an ellipse whose semi-axes are the singular values.
6.8 The SVD is connected to the symmetric matrices A transpose A and A A transpose. The right singular vectors are the eigenvectors of A transpose A, the left singular vectors are the eigenvectors of A A transpose, and the squares of the singular values are the eigenvalues of both. The first r columns of V and U provide orthonormal bases for the row space and column space, and the remaining columns for the nullspace and left nullspace: the SVD displays all four fundamental subspaces at once.
6.9 The SVD writes A as a sum of rank-one pieces: sigma one u one v one transpose, plus sigma two u two v two transpose, and so on, in order of importance. Keeping only the first k pieces gives the best possible rank-k approximation to A, a result known as the Eckart-Young theorem, published by Carl Eckart and Gale Young in 1936. If the singular values fall off quickly, a few pieces capture almost all of the matrix.
6.10 Image compression illustrates this. A greyscale image is a matrix of pixel brightnesses. Its singular values typically decrease rapidly, so keeping the largest few dozen rank-one pieces of a large image gives a recognisable picture while storing far fewer numbers. Practical formats such as JPEG use a fixed cosine basis instead, which is faster, but the SVD shows the best that any rank-limited approximation can do.
6.11 In data analysis, the SVD powers principal component analysis, or PCA. Arrange measurements in a matrix with one row per variable and one column per sample, subtract each variable's mean, and compute the SVD. The first left singular vector is the direction of greatest variation in the data, the second the next greatest perpendicular direction, and so on. Projecting onto the first few principal components reduces many correlated variables to a few that capture most of the variation, a method introduced by Karl Pearson in 1901.
6.12 PCA is used across engineering and science: to find the dominant patterns in sensor data from a machine, to identify the main modes of variation in measured shapes, to reduce gene-expression data from thousands of genes to a few components, and to compress data before machine learning. Engineering 509 B, Probability and Statistics, will connect it to the covariance matrix of the data, whose eigenvectors are the principal components.
6.13 The SVD also solves least-squares problems robustly. When A has dependent or nearly dependent columns, the normal equations fail or become unreliable, but the pseudoinverse, built from the SVD by inverting only the non-zero, or not-too-small, singular values, gives the shortest least-squares solution. The ratio of largest to smallest singular value is the condition number of lecture two, which measures how much a problem amplifies errors.
6.14 It is worth pausing to see how far the course has come. We began with linear combinations and A x equals b, solved it by elimination and L U, understood its solutions through the four subspaces, handled impossible systems by projection and least squares, and analysed matrices through determinants, eigenvalues, and finally the SVD. Each factorisation, L U, Q R, X Lambda X inverse, Q Lambda Q transpose, and U Sigma V transpose, reveals a different aspect of the same matrix.
6.15 In engineering practice, nearly all of this runs inside software libraries, such as LAPACK, used by MATLAB, NumPy, and most simulation programs. The engineer's job is to formulate the model as a linear algebra problem, to know which factorisation fits, and to interpret the answers: whether a matrix is singular or ill-conditioned, whether a structure is stable, which modes dominate, and what the data really contain. Machine learning, too, is linear algebra at enormous scale, with matrices multiplied billions of times.
