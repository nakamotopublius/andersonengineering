@L 0 | Course Overview | What biology is, why engineers study it, how this Brief course is laid out, and where it comes from.
0.1 Welcome to Anderson Academy. This is Engineering 506 B, Introductory Biology, the Foundations course in biology for Anderson Engineering. It is a Brief course: this overview of about five minutes, then six lectures of about ten minutes each, so about an hour in all. The letter B at the end of the course code stands for Brief. The department code is ENGR, which is spoken as Engineering, so this course is Engineering 506 B.
0.2 Questions or feedback are always welcome, by email to garyanderson@mail.grokbot.com or on X at @garyanderson.
0.3 Biology is the science of living things. For most of history it was a science of description: naming species, drawing anatomy, and recording behaviour. Over the last century it became a molecular science. We now understand life as chemistry organised by information: molecules that store instructions, molecules that read them, and molecules that carry them out. That change is why MIT requires every undergraduate, including every engineer, to take one subject in introductory biology.
0.4 Engineers meet biology everywhere. Biomedical and biological engineers design drugs, devices, and diagnostic tests. Chemical engineers grow cells in bioreactors to make medicines and fuels. Civil and environmental engineers treat wastewater with microbes. Computer scientists analyse genomes. Mechanical engineers study how cells sense force. Even engineers who never touch a cell benefit from biology's way of thinking about complex systems that build and repair themselves.
0.5 Here is the map of this course, which follows the shared core of MIT's introductory biology subjects, numbered 7.012, 7.013, 7.014, 7.015, and 7.016. Lecture one introduces the molecules of life: water, carbon, and the four great classes of biological molecules, with special attention to proteins. Lecture two describes the cell, its membranes, enzymes, and how cells capture and use energy. Lecture three is genetics: how traits are inherited, from Mendel's peas to chromosomes.
0.6 Lecture four explains DNA and the central dogma of molecular biology: how DNA is copied, how genes are transcribed into RNA and translated into protein, and how genes are switched on and off. Lecture five covers the tools of molecular biology and biotechnology: cutting and copying DNA, sequencing genomes, and editing genes. Lecture six looks at how cells communicate, how the cell cycle goes wrong in cancer, how the immune system defends the body, and how evolution ties all of biology together.
0.7 The prerequisite is Engineering 505 B, Chemistry, or an equivalent course. You will need the ideas of covalent and hydrogen bonds, polarity, free energy, and catalysts. No earlier biology course is assumed. The mathematics in this course is light; the challenge is the number of new names, so learn them in families rather than as isolated words.
0.8 Because this is a Brief course, every lecture chooses its examples carefully and leaves a great deal out. There is no laboratory work in this course, and nothing in it qualifies you to work with living organisms, human samples, or hazardous biological materials. Biology is also a field where much is still unknown; when this course says how something works, it describes the established core, not the latest research.

@L 1 | The Molecules of Life | Water and carbon, the four classes of biological macromolecules, how they are built and broken down, and the structure of proteins.
1.1 All known life is built mainly from six elements: carbon, hydrogen, nitrogen, oxygen, phosphorus, and sulphur. Living things are mostly water, around sixty per cent of the human body by mass. Water matters because of its polarity and hydrogen bonding, the chemistry of Engineering 505 B. It dissolves charged and polar molecules, it stores heat well and so steadies temperature, and its tendency to exclude non-polar molecules drives much of biological structure.
1.2 Molecules that dissolve readily in water are called hydrophilic, water-loving; those that do not, such as oils, are hydrophobic, water-fearing. When non-polar molecules are placed in water, they cluster together, not because they strongly attract one another but because this lets the surrounding water form more hydrogen bonds. This hydrophobic effect folds proteins and assembles cell membranes. It is one of the most important organising forces in biology.
1.3 Carbon is the backbone element of life because each carbon atom forms four covalent bonds, allowing chains, branches, and rings of almost unlimited variety. Attached to these carbon skeletons are functional groups that give molecules their chemical character: hydroxyl groups, which are polar; carboxyl groups, which are acidic; amino groups, which are basic; and phosphate groups, which carry negative charge and appear in DNA and in the energy carrier ATP.
1.4 Most large biological molecules, called macromolecules, are polymers: long chains of similar small units called monomers. Cells build polymers by dehydration reactions, which join two monomers and release a molecule of water, and break them down by hydrolysis, which adds water to split the bond. Building polymers requires energy; breaking them releases it. There are four main classes of biological molecules: carbohydrates, lipids, proteins, and nucleic acids.
1.5 Carbohydrates are sugars and their polymers. Glucose, with six carbons, is the central fuel of most cells. Joined in long chains, glucose forms starch in plants and glycogen in animals, both used to store energy. Joined with a slightly different bond geometry, glucose forms cellulose, the structural fibre of plant cell walls and the most abundant organic molecule on Earth. Humans cannot digest cellulose, which is why the same sugar can be food in one polymer and roughage in another.
1.6 Lipids are a diverse group united by being mostly hydrophobic. Fats are made of glycerol joined to three fatty acid chains and store about twice as much energy per gram as carbohydrates. Phospholipids have two fatty acid tails and a charged phosphate head, so one end is hydrophobic and the other hydrophilic. In water they spontaneously form a double layer, the lipid bilayer, with tails inside and heads facing the water. Every cell membrane is built on this bilayer. Steroids such as cholesterol and some hormones are also lipids.
1.7 Proteins do most of the work of the cell. They are catalysts, structural fibres, motors, pumps, channels, receptors, antibodies, and signals. A protein is a polymer of amino acids, joined by peptide bonds into a chain called a polypeptide. There are twenty standard amino acids. Each has the same backbone, an amino group, a carboxyl group, and a central carbon, but a different side chain, called the R group, which may be non-polar, polar, acidic, or basic.
1.8 The sequence of amino acids in a chain is the protein's primary structure. That sequence folds into a precise three-dimensional shape, and the shape determines the function. Biologists describe four levels of structure. Primary structure is the sequence. Secondary structure consists of local patterns held by hydrogen bonds between backbone atoms, chiefly the alpha helix and the beta sheet. Tertiary structure is the overall fold of one chain. Quaternary structure is the assembly of several chains, as in haemoglobin, which has four.
1.9 What drives folding? Largely the hydrophobic effect: non-polar side chains bury themselves in the protein's core, away from water, while polar and charged side chains face outward. Hydrogen bonds, ionic attractions, and in some proteins covalent disulphide bridges between cysteine side chains add stability. In 1961 Christian Anfinsen showed that a denatured enzyme could refold correctly by itself, demonstrating that the sequence alone contains the information for the shape.
1.10 Because function depends on shape, a small change in sequence can matter enormously. Sickle-cell disease is caused by a single amino acid substitution in haemoglobin: a valine in place of a glutamic acid at one position in the beta chain. The changed molecules stick together when oxygen is low, distorting red blood cells into a sickle shape. Heat, extreme pH, or certain chemicals can also unfold, or denature, proteins, which is why a fever that is too high is dangerous and why cooking changes an egg white.
1.11 Predicting a protein's shape from its sequence was one of biology's great unsolved problems for half a century. Experimental methods such as X-ray crystallography, the technique from Engineering 505 B, and later cryo-electron microscopy determined structures one at a time. In 2020 the machine-learning system AlphaFold, developed at DeepMind, began predicting many structures with accuracy close to experiment, a result recognised with a share of the 2024 Nobel Prize in Chemistry.
1.12 Nucleic acids store and transmit hereditary information. There are two kinds: deoxyribonucleic acid, DNA, and ribonucleic acid, RNA. Both are polymers of nucleotides. Each nucleotide has three parts: a five-carbon sugar, a phosphate group, and a nitrogen-containing base. The sugar is deoxyribose in DNA and ribose in RNA. The sugars and phosphates link into a backbone, and the bases stick out from it, carrying the information.
1.13 DNA uses four bases: adenine, guanine, cytosine, and thymine, abbreviated A, G, C, and T. RNA uses uracil, U, in place of thymine. In DNA, two strands wind around each other in the double helix described by James Watson and Francis Crick in 1953, drawing on X-ray images made by Rosalind Franklin and Maurice Wilkins. The bases pair across the helix by hydrogen bonds: A always with T, and G always with C. That rule, complementary base pairing, is the key to copying and reading genetic information.
1.14 The two strands of DNA run in opposite directions, a property called antiparallel. The ends of each strand are named five prime and three prime after the numbering of the sugar's carbon atoms. This direction matters: the enzymes that copy and read DNA work in only one direction along a strand. We will use this fact in lecture four.
1.15 Small molecules matter too. Adenosine triphosphate, ATP, is a nucleotide with three phosphates in a row. Removing the last phosphate by hydrolysis releases free energy, which the cell couples to work it could not otherwise do, from muscle contraction to building proteins. ATP is often called the energy currency of the cell: it is constantly spent and regenerated, and a human body turns over roughly its own weight in ATP each day.
1.16 To summarise: life is water-based carbon chemistry. Four classes of macromolecules, carbohydrates, lipids, proteins, and nucleic acids, are built from monomers by dehydration and broken by hydrolysis. Proteins fold into shapes set by their sequences, and shape determines function. Nucleic acids store information in a sequence of bases that pair by a simple rule. Next we see how these molecules are organised into cells.

@L 2 | Cells, Membranes, and Energy | The cell theory, prokaryotic and eukaryotic cells, organelles, membranes and transport, enzymes, and how cells harvest energy through respiration and photosynthesis.
2.1 The cell is the smallest unit of life. The cell theory, developed in the nineteenth century by Matthias Schleiden, Theodor Schwann, and Rudolf Virchow, states that all living things are made of cells, that the cell is the basic unit of structure and function, and that all cells come from pre-existing cells. Some organisms, such as bacteria and yeast, are single cells; a human body contains tens of trillions of cells of a few hundred types.
2.2 Cells come in two basic designs. Prokaryotic cells, the bacteria and archaea, have no nucleus; their DNA lies in a region of the cytoplasm, usually as a single circular chromosome. They are small, typically one to a few micrometres across. Eukaryotic cells, found in animals, plants, fungi, and protists, keep their DNA in a membrane-bound nucleus and contain other membrane-bound compartments called organelles. They are typically ten to a hundred micrometres across.
2.3 The organelles of a eukaryotic cell divide the work. The nucleus holds the chromosomes. The endoplasmic reticulum, a folded membrane network, makes lipids and many proteins; ribosomes on its surface give rough endoplasmic reticulum its name. The Golgi apparatus modifies, sorts, and ships proteins. Lysosomes digest worn-out components. Mitochondria release energy from food. Plant cells add chloroplasts for photosynthesis, a large central vacuole, and a rigid cell wall of cellulose. A cytoskeleton of protein filaments gives shape and moves things.
2.4 Mitochondria and chloroplasts have their own small circular DNA and their own ribosomes, which resemble those of bacteria. This evidence supports the endosymbiotic theory, championed by Lynn Margulis: these organelles descend from free-living bacteria that were engulfed by an ancestral cell over a billion years ago and became permanent partners. It is a striking example of how evolution builds complexity by combining existing parts.
2.5 Every cell is enclosed by a plasma membrane: a lipid bilayer, about five nanometres thick, studded with proteins. The fluid mosaic model, proposed by Seymour Singer and Garth Nicolson in 1972, describes the membrane as a two-dimensional fluid in which lipids and many proteins drift laterally. The bilayer's hydrophobic core is a barrier to ions and most polar molecules, so the membrane lets the cell keep an internal chemistry very different from its surroundings.
2.6 Some small molecules cross the membrane by simple diffusion, moving down their concentration gradient without help; oxygen and carbon dioxide are examples. Water crosses by osmosis, moving toward the side with the higher concentration of dissolved particles, often through channel proteins called aquaporins. Ions and polar molecules such as glucose need protein channels or carriers; when they move down their gradients this way, it is called facilitated diffusion, and it requires no energy.
2.7 Active transport moves substances against their concentration gradients and requires energy, usually from ATP. The sodium-potassium pump, discovered by Jens Christian Skou, uses one ATP to pump three sodium ions out of the cell and two potassium ions in. The resulting gradients store energy, like water held behind a dam, and they power nerve impulses and the uptake of nutrients. A large fraction of the energy used by the human brain goes to running this pump.
2.8 Almost every chemical reaction in a cell is catalysed by an enzyme, usually a protein. As Engineering 505 B explained, a catalyst lowers the activation energy without changing the free-energy difference or the equilibrium. An enzyme binds its reactant, called the substrate, in a pocket called the active site, whose shape and chemistry suit that substrate. Enzymes can speed reactions by factors of a million or more, and they are highly specific.
2.9 Enzyme activity depends on conditions. Each enzyme has an optimum temperature and pH; most human enzymes work best near thirty-seven degrees Celsius and near neutral pH, though the stomach enzyme pepsin works best in strong acid. Inhibitors reduce activity: a competitive inhibitor occupies the active site, while other inhibitors bind elsewhere and change the enzyme's shape. Cells regulate pathways by feedback inhibition, in which the final product of a pathway inhibits an enzyme near its start. Many drugs are enzyme inhibitors.
2.10 Cells obey the laws of thermodynamics. Building ordered structures decreases entropy locally, so it must be paid for with free energy. Cells do this by coupling unfavourable reactions to favourable ones, most often the hydrolysis of ATP. Metabolism is the whole network of these reactions: catabolic pathways that break down molecules and release energy, and anabolic pathways that build molecules and consume it.
2.11 Cellular respiration releases the energy stored in glucose. The overall reaction is glucose plus six oxygen giving six carbon dioxide plus six water, with a large negative free energy change. The cell releases this energy in many small steps, capturing it in ATP rather than as heat. The first stage, glycolysis, happens in the cytoplasm and splits glucose into two molecules of pyruvate, yielding a small net gain of two ATP. It needs no oxygen.
2.12 In the presence of oxygen, pyruvate enters the mitochondria. The citric acid cycle, also called the Krebs cycle after Hans Krebs, oxidises it completely to carbon dioxide and loads the released electrons onto carrier molecules, NADH and FADH two. These carriers deliver the electrons to the electron transport chain in the inner mitochondrial membrane, where they pass down a series of proteins to oxygen, the final acceptor, forming water. Without oxygen, cells fall back on fermentation, which makes much less ATP.
2.13 The electron transport chain does not make ATP directly. Instead it pumps protons across the inner membrane, building a gradient of concentration and charge. The protons flow back through an enzyme called ATP synthase, a rotary molecular motor, which uses their energy to make ATP. This mechanism, called chemiosmosis, was proposed by Peter Mitchell in 1961. In total, one glucose yields roughly thirty to thirty-two ATP, most of it from this stage.
2.14 Photosynthesis runs the overall process in reverse, using light. In chloroplasts, the light-dependent reactions absorb light with chlorophyll, split water, release oxygen, and produce ATP and NADPH, again by a proton gradient and ATP synthase. The light-independent reactions, the Calvin cycle, use that ATP and NADPH to fix carbon dioxide into sugar. The overall reaction is six carbon dioxide plus six water plus light energy giving glucose plus six oxygen.
2.15 Photosynthesis and respiration together form a global cycle. Photosynthetic organisms on land and in the oceans capture carbon dioxide and release oxygen; nearly all organisms respire, consuming oxygen and releasing carbon dioxide. Microscopic ocean phytoplankton carry out roughly half of the world's photosynthesis. Fossil fuels are ancient photosynthetic products, and burning them returns long-stored carbon to the atmosphere.
2.16 To summarise: the cell is the unit of life. Prokaryotes lack a nucleus; eukaryotes have one and many organelles. Membranes are lipid bilayers with proteins that control transport, passive and active. Enzymes catalyse reactions, and the cell harvests energy by respiration, using proton gradients and ATP synthase, while photosynthesis captures energy from light. Next we turn to how cells pass on their instructions.

@L 3 | Genetics and Inheritance | Mendel's laws, alleles, dominance, genotype and phenotype, chromosomes, mitosis and meiosis, sex linkage, linkage and recombination, and pedigrees.
3.1 Genetics is the study of heredity: why offspring resemble their parents, and why they also differ. Before anyone knew of DNA, the Moravian friar Gregor Mendel worked out its basic rules by breeding garden peas in his monastery garden. He published his results in 1866, but their importance was not recognised until 1900, when three other researchers independently rediscovered them.
3.2 Mendel chose traits that came in two clear forms, such as round or wrinkled seeds and purple or white flowers, and he started from true-breeding lines. When he crossed a purple-flowered plant with a white-flowered one, all the offspring, the first generation, had purple flowers. When he let those plants self-fertilise, about three quarters of the next generation were purple and one quarter white: a three-to-one ratio. The white trait had not been lost; it had been hidden.
3.3 Mendel's explanation, in modern words, is this. Each plant carries two copies of a hereditary factor for each trait, now called a gene. Different versions of a gene are called alleles. One allele can be dominant over another, called recessive, so that a plant with one copy of each shows the dominant form. Each parent passes on one of its two alleles, chosen at random, to each offspring. This is Mendel's first law, the law of segregation.
3.4 Geneticists distinguish genotype, the alleles an individual carries, from phenotype, the trait it shows. Write P for the dominant purple allele and p for the recessive white one. A PP plant is homozygous dominant and purple; a Pp plant is heterozygous and also purple; a pp plant is homozygous recessive and white. A Punnett square, named after Reginald Punnett, lists the possible gametes of each parent along two sides and fills in the combinations, showing why a Pp by Pp cross gives one PP, two Pp, and one pp: three purple to one white.
3.5 Mendel also followed two traits at once, such as seed shape and seed colour. Crossing plants heterozygous for both gave a ratio of nine to three to three to one among the four combinations of traits. This showed that alleles of different genes were sorted into gametes independently of one another, Mendel's second law, the law of independent assortment. As we will see, this law holds only for genes on different chromosomes, or far apart on the same one.
3.6 Not all traits follow simple dominance. In incomplete dominance, the heterozygote is intermediate, as in snapdragons where red and white parents give pink offspring. In codominance, both alleles show fully, as in the human AB blood group, where the A and B alleles each produce their own surface molecule. Many traits, such as height, are polygenic, shaped by many genes plus the environment, and they vary continuously rather than in clear classes.
3.7 Where are genes physically? Early in the twentieth century, Walter Sutton and Theodor Boveri proposed that genes are carried on chromosomes, the thread-like structures visible in dividing cells. Chromosomes come in pairs, one from each parent, and they separate when sex cells form, exactly as Mendel's factors should. Human body cells have forty-six chromosomes, twenty-three pairs: twenty-two pairs of autosomes and one pair of sex chromosomes, XX in typical females and XY in typical males.
3.8 Before a cell divides it must copy its chromosomes. Each copied chromosome consists of two identical sister chromatids joined at a constriction called the centromere. In mitosis, the ordinary division of body cells, the sister chromatids are pulled apart by a spindle of microtubules, and each daughter cell receives one full set. Mitosis produces two genetically identical cells and is how a body grows and repairs itself. Its main stages are prophase, metaphase, anaphase, and telophase.
3.9 Meiosis is the special division that makes eggs and sperm. It has two rounds of division after one round of copying, so it produces four cells, each with one chromosome from every pair: twenty-three in humans rather than forty-six. These cells are haploid; body cells, with two sets, are diploid. Fertilisation joins two haploid gametes to restore the diploid number. In the first meiotic division, the paired chromosomes of each pair line up and separate, which is the physical basis of Mendel's law of segregation.
3.10 Meiosis creates variation in two ways. First, each chromosome pair lines up independently, so in humans a single parent can produce two to the power twenty-three, about eight million, different combinations of chromosomes. That is independent assortment. Second, before they separate, paired chromosomes exchange segments in a process called crossing over, mixing alleles along a chromosome. Together with random fertilisation, this makes every child, apart from identical twins, genetically unique.
3.11 Genes on the same chromosome tend to be inherited together, a situation called linkage, unless crossing over separates them. Thomas Hunt Morgan and his students, working with fruit flies at Columbia University from about 1910, discovered linkage and realised that the frequency of recombination between two genes reflects the distance between them. In 1913 Morgan's student Alfred Sturtevant used recombination frequencies to draw the first genetic map. One per cent recombination is defined as one map unit, or one centimorgan.
3.12 Morgan also discovered sex linkage. A white-eye mutation in fruit flies appeared mostly in males, because the gene lies on the X chromosome. Males have only one X, so a single recessive allele shows. In humans, red-green colour blindness and haemophilia A are X-linked recessive conditions, much more common in males. A carrier mother passes the allele to half her sons on average, who will be affected, and to half her daughters, who will usually be carriers.
3.13 For humans, geneticists cannot arrange crosses, so they study pedigrees, family trees that record who shows a trait. An autosomal recessive trait, such as cystic fibrosis, can skip generations and appear in children of two unaffected carrier parents, one quarter of whom will be affected on average. An autosomal dominant trait, such as Huntington's disease, typically appears in every generation, and an affected heterozygous parent passes it to half the children on average.
3.14 Chromosome-level errors also occur. If a chromosome pair fails to separate during meiosis, an event called nondisjunction, a gamete may carry an extra chromosome or lack one. Down syndrome usually results from three copies of chromosome twenty-one. Most other such errors are not compatible with development. Such abnormalities become more frequent with maternal age, which is why prenatal screening is often offered to older parents.
3.15 Probability is the natural language of genetics. Each child's inheritance is an independent random event, so the product rule applies: the probability that two independent things both happen is the product of their probabilities. For two carrier parents, the chance that two children are both affected by a recessive condition is one quarter times one quarter, one sixteenth. Engineering 509 B, Probability and Statistics, develops these tools properly.
3.16 To summarise: Mendel discovered that inheritance works through discrete factors, now called genes, which come in alleles that segregate and, for unlinked genes, assort independently. Genes lie on chromosomes. Mitosis copies cells exactly; meiosis halves the chromosome number and shuffles alleles by independent assortment and crossing over. Linkage, sex linkage, and pedigrees extend Mendel's rules. Next we ask what a gene actually is.

@L 4 | DNA, Genes, and the Central Dogma | Evidence that DNA is the genetic material, DNA replication, transcription, the genetic code and translation, mutations, and the regulation of gene expression.
4.1 By the 1940s, chromosomes were known to contain both protein and DNA, and many scientists expected the more varied proteins to carry genetic information. Two experiments pointed to DNA instead. In 1944 Oswald Avery, Colin MacLeod, and Maclyn McCarty showed that purified DNA, not protein, could transform harmless bacteria into a disease-causing strain. In 1952 Alfred Hershey and Martha Chase showed that when a bacterial virus infects a cell, it is the virus's DNA, not its protein coat, that enters and directs the making of new viruses.
4.2 The double helix of 1953 immediately suggested how DNA could be copied. Because A pairs with T and G with C, each strand determines the sequence of its partner. Separate the strands and use each as a template for a new partner, and you get two identical double helices. Each new helix contains one old strand and one new strand, a pattern called semiconservative replication. Matthew Meselson and Franklin Stahl confirmed it in 1958 with an elegant experiment using heavy and light isotopes of nitrogen.
4.3 Replication is carried out by a team of enzymes. Helicase unwinds the helix. Primase lays down a short RNA primer. DNA polymerase then adds nucleotides one at a time, but only to the three-prime end of a growing strand, so new DNA is always built in the five-prime to three-prime direction. One strand, the leading strand, is made continuously; the other, the lagging strand, is made in short pieces called Okazaki fragments, later joined by DNA ligase.
4.4 Replication is remarkably accurate. DNA polymerase checks each new base and removes most mistakes, a step called proofreading, and other repair systems fix many of the errors that remain. The final error rate in human cells is roughly one mistake per billion bases copied. That accuracy matters, since a human cell copies about six billion bases, both sets of chromosomes, every time it divides.
4.5 What does DNA encode? In 1941 George Beadle and Edward Tatum, studying a bread mould, concluded that each gene directs the making of one enzyme, later refined to one polypeptide. Francis Crick summarised the flow of information in 1958 as the central dogma of molecular biology: DNA is transcribed into RNA, and RNA is translated into protein. Information flows from nucleic acid to protein, not back again.
4.6 Transcription copies the information in a gene into a molecule of messenger RNA, or mRNA. The enzyme RNA polymerase binds to a DNA sequence called a promoter at the start of the gene, unwinds a short stretch, and builds an RNA strand complementary to one DNA strand, the template strand, using U in place of T. In eukaryotes, the initial RNA is processed before leaving the nucleus: a cap and a tail are added, and non-coding segments called introns are cut out, while the coding segments, called exons, are spliced together.
4.7 Translation turns the sequence of mRNA into a sequence of amino acids. The mRNA is read in groups of three bases, called codons. With four bases there are four times four times four, sixty-four, possible codons. Sixty-one specify amino acids, and three are stop signals. The codon AUG specifies methionine and also marks the start of most proteins. Since there are sixty-one amino-acid codons for twenty amino acids, most amino acids have several codons; the code is said to be redundant, or degenerate.
4.8 The genetic code was cracked in the 1960s, beginning with Marshall Nirenberg and Heinrich Matthaei's 1961 finding that an artificial RNA made only of U produces a protein made only of phenylalanine, so UUU codes for phenylalanine. Har Gobind Khorana and others completed the table. The code is nearly universal: the same codons mean the same amino acids in bacteria, plants, and people. That universality is strong evidence of common ancestry, and it is what makes biotechnology possible.
4.9 Translation takes place on ribosomes, large machines made of RNA and protein. Transfer RNA molecules, or tRNAs, act as adapters. Each tRNA carries a specific amino acid at one end and has, at the other end, three bases called an anticodon that pair with a matching codon on the mRNA. The ribosome moves along the mRNA codon by codon, bringing in the matching tRNAs and joining their amino acids into a growing chain, until it reaches a stop codon and releases the finished polypeptide.
4.10 A mutation is a change in the DNA sequence. A point mutation changes one base. It may be silent, if the new codon means the same amino acid; missense, if it changes the amino acid, as in sickle-cell disease; or nonsense, if it creates a stop codon and cuts the protein short. Insertions or deletions of bases that are not a multiple of three cause frameshift mutations, which scramble every codon downstream and usually destroy the protein's function.
4.11 Mutations arise from copying errors and from damage by chemicals and radiation, such as ultraviolet light, which can fuse adjacent thymines. Most mutations are neutral or harmful, but a few are beneficial, and mutation is the ultimate source of all genetic variation. A mutation in a body cell affects only that cell's descendants and may contribute to cancer; a mutation in a cell that makes eggs or sperm can be passed to the next generation.
4.12 Every cell in a body carries essentially the same DNA, yet a nerve cell and a liver cell are very different. The difference is gene expression: which genes are transcribed and translated, and how much. Gene regulation is how cells respond to their environment and how a single fertilised egg develops into a body with many cell types. Control can act at transcription, at RNA processing, at translation, or on the finished protein, but transcription is the main control point.
4.13 The classic example comes from bacteria. In 1961 François Jacob and Jacques Monod described the lac operon of the bacterium Escherichia coli, a cluster of genes for using the sugar lactose, transcribed together from one promoter. When lactose is absent, a repressor protein binds to a DNA site called the operator and blocks transcription. When lactose is present, a form of it binds the repressor and releases it, and the genes are switched on. A second control turns the operon up only when glucose, the preferred fuel, is scarce.
4.14 Eukaryotic regulation is more elaborate. Proteins called transcription factors bind to DNA sequences near genes, and to enhancers that may lie thousands of bases away, to recruit or block RNA polymerase. DNA in the nucleus is wrapped around proteins called histones, forming chromatin, and chemical tags on the histones and on the DNA itself can make genes more or less accessible. Such heritable changes in gene activity that do not alter the sequence are studied in epigenetics.
4.15 Small RNAs also regulate genes. MicroRNAs, discovered in the worm Caenorhabditis elegans by Victor Ambros and Gary Ruvkun, bind to matching mRNAs and reduce their translation. Their discovery was recognised with the 2024 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine. Related mechanisms are used in the laboratory to silence chosen genes. They show that RNA is not merely a messenger but an active part of the cell's control system.
4.16 To summarise: DNA is the genetic material. It is copied semiconservatively by DNA polymerase, five prime to three prime, with proofreading. Genes are transcribed into RNA and translated into protein by ribosomes and tRNAs using a nearly universal triplet code. Mutations change sequences and supply variation, and gene regulation, chiefly at transcription, decides which genes each cell uses. Next we see how scientists read, copy, and edit DNA.

@L 5 | Molecular Biology and Biotechnology | Restriction enzymes and recombinant DNA, the polymerase chain reaction, DNA sequencing and genomics, gene editing with CRISPR, and biology as an engineering discipline.
5.1 Once the central dogma was understood, biologists learned to manipulate DNA directly. The first key tools were restriction enzymes, found in bacteria, where they defend against viruses by cutting foreign DNA. Each restriction enzyme recognises a specific short sequence, often four to eight bases long and reading the same on both strands, and cuts the DNA there. The enzyme EcoRI, for example, cuts at GAATTC. Many enzymes leave short single-stranded overhangs, called sticky ends.
5.2 Any two pieces of DNA cut with the same enzyme have matching sticky ends, which can pair and then be sealed by DNA ligase. In 1973 Stanley Cohen and Herbert Boyer used this to insert a foreign gene into a plasmid, a small circular DNA molecule that bacteria copy independently of their chromosome. Bacteria that took up the recombinant plasmid copied the foreign gene as they multiplied. This was the beginning of recombinant DNA technology and of the biotechnology industry.
5.3 Gene cloning in this sense means making many copies of a piece of DNA in a host. A cloning plasmid usually carries an antibiotic-resistance gene, so that only bacteria containing the plasmid survive on a medium with that antibiotic. If the plasmid also carries signals for expression, the bacteria will make the protein encoded by the inserted gene. Human insulin made in bacteria, approved in 1982, was the first medicine produced this way, replacing insulin purified from animal pancreases.
5.4 Gel electrophoresis separates DNA fragments by size. DNA is negatively charged because of its phosphate backbone, so in an electric field it moves toward the positive electrode. Through a gel, small fragments move faster than large ones. After running, the DNA is stained, and the fragments appear as bands whose positions reveal their lengths when compared with a ladder of known sizes. This simple method appears in nearly every molecular biology laboratory.
5.5 The polymerase chain reaction, PCR, invented by Kary Mullis in 1983, copies a chosen stretch of DNA millions of times in a test tube. Each cycle has three steps: heat to about ninety-five degrees Celsius to separate the strands, cool to let two short primers bind to the ends of the target sequence, then warm to about seventy-two degrees so a DNA polymerase extends the primers. Each cycle doubles the number of target copies, so thirty cycles can give about a billion copies from a single molecule.
5.6 PCR became practical because of a heat-stable DNA polymerase, Taq polymerase, from the bacterium Thermus aquaticus, discovered in a hot spring in Yellowstone National Park. Because it survives the heating steps, the reaction can run automatically in a programmable heating block. PCR is used in diagnostics, forensics, ancient DNA research, and paternity testing. During the COVID-19 pandemic, a variant called reverse-transcription PCR, which first copies viral RNA into DNA, became the standard test.
5.7 DNA sequencing determines the order of bases. In 1977 Frederick Sanger developed the chain-termination method. A polymerase copies the DNA in the presence of a small amount of modified nucleotides that stop the growing strand when added. This produces fragments ending at every position, which are separated by size, and the terminating base at each length reveals the sequence. Sanger sequencing, later automated with fluorescent dyes, was the workhorse of genomics for three decades.
5.8 The Human Genome Project, an international effort, produced a reference sequence of the human genome, completed in 2003. The genome has about three billion base pairs per set of chromosomes but only about twenty thousand protein-coding genes, far fewer than expected. Protein-coding sequences make up only about one to two per cent of the genome; the rest includes regulatory sequences, RNA genes, and repetitive elements. Any two people's genomes are about ninety-nine point nine per cent identical.
5.9 Newer sequencing technologies, often called next-generation sequencing, read millions of short fragments at once in parallel, and computers assemble them. The cost of sequencing a human genome has fallen from billions of dollars for the first one to the order of hundreds of dollars today, faster than the improvement of computer chips over the same period. Genomics now routinely compares thousands of genomes to find genes linked to disease, to trace outbreaks, and to classify tumours.
5.10 Genomics is also an information science. A single genome produces gigabytes of raw data, and a hospital or research centre may handle petabytes. Algorithms align reads, assemble genomes, and search for meaningful patterns. Statistics, the subject of Engineering 509 B, is needed to distinguish real associations from chance among millions of tests. Linear algebra, the subject of Engineering 508 B, underlies many methods for analysing gene-expression data across thousands of genes at once.
5.11 Gene editing changes DNA at a chosen site inside living cells. The most widely used system, CRISPR-Cas9, was adapted from a bacterial immune system. A guide RNA, about twenty bases long, matches the target sequence and leads the Cas9 protein there, and Cas9 cuts both strands. The cell then repairs the cut. Imprecise repair often disables the gene; if a template is supplied, the cell may copy it in, making a precise change. Jennifer Doudna and Emmanuelle Charpentier described the method in 2012 and received the 2020 Nobel Prize in Chemistry.
5.12 CRISPR has transformed research, because changing a gene and observing the effect is now quick and cheap in many organisms. It has also reached medicine: in late 2023 and early 2024 regulators in the United Kingdom and the United States approved a CRISPR-based therapy for sickle-cell disease and beta thalassaemia, in which a patient's own blood stem cells are edited outside the body and returned. Edits to embryos, which would be inherited, are widely considered unacceptable at present, and they are prohibited or restricted in many countries.
5.13 Other biotechnologies build on the same foundations. Monoclonal antibodies, identical antibodies produced in large quantities, are among the best-selling medicines. Messenger RNA vaccines deliver an mRNA that instructs the body's cells to make a viral protein, prompting immunity; the COVID-19 vaccines of 2020 were the first widely used examples. Genetically modified crops carry genes for resistance to pests or herbicides.
5.14 Biology has become an engineering discipline. Synthetic biologists design genetic circuits from standardised parts, such as promoters, genes, and terminators, much as electrical engineers design circuits from components. Early landmark examples, both published in 2000, were a genetic toggle switch, built by Timothy Gardner, Charles Cantor, and James Collins, and a genetic oscillator called the repressilator, built by Michael Elowitz and Stanislas Leibler. Engineered microbes now produce an antimalarial drug precursor, artemisinic acid, and many industrial chemicals.
5.15 Power brings responsibility. Biotechnology raises questions of safety, such as the containment of engineered organisms, and of security, such as the misuse of pathogen research. It raises ethical questions about genetic privacy, about access to expensive therapies, and about the limits of changing human heredity. The 1975 Asilomar conference, at which scientists agreed on early safeguards for recombinant DNA, is often cited as an example of a field governing itself before regulators were ready.
5.16 To summarise: restriction enzymes and ligase make recombinant DNA; plasmids and bacteria clone it; electrophoresis sorts it by size; PCR amplifies it; sequencing reads it; and CRISPR edits it. Genomics turned biology into an information science, and synthetic biology treats living systems as things that can be designed. Next we look at how cells talk to one another, what goes wrong in cancer, how the body defends itself, and how evolution explains it all.

@L 6 | Signalling, Cancer, Immunity, and Evolution | How cells communicate, the cell cycle and its control, cancer as a disease of genes, the immune system, and evolution by natural selection as the unifying theory of biology.
6.1 The cells of a multicellular organism must coordinate their behaviour, and they do it by signalling. A signalling cell releases a molecule, such as a hormone, a growth factor, or a neurotransmitter. A target cell detects it with a receptor protein, often on its surface. The receptor changes shape and triggers a chain of events inside the cell, called signal transduction, that ends in a response: switching genes on, changing metabolism, moving, dividing, or dying.
6.2 Signal transduction pathways amplify and relay signals. A common pattern begins with a receptor that, when bound by its signal, activates an enzyme inside the cell. That enzyme may make a small messenger molecule, such as cyclic AMP, or may add phosphate groups to other proteins, a modification called phosphorylation that switches them on or off. Enzymes that add phosphates are called kinases. A cascade of kinases, each activating many copies of the next, can turn a few signal molecules into a large response.
6.3 Signals act at different ranges. Hormones such as insulin travel through the blood to distant tissues; insulin, released by the pancreas when blood sugar rises, tells muscle and fat cells to take up glucose. Local signals act on nearby cells. Nerve cells signal across tiny gaps called synapses using neurotransmitters, with electrical impulses carrying the message along each nerve cell, powered by the ion gradients described in lecture two.
6.4 One of the most important decisions a cell makes is whether to divide. The cell cycle has four phases: G one, a growth phase; S, when DNA is synthesised; G two, a second growth phase; and M, mitosis. At checkpoints the cell assesses whether conditions are right to proceed, for example whether DNA is intact before it is copied and whether chromosomes are attached to the spindle before they separate. Proteins called cyclins and cyclin-dependent kinases drive the cell from one phase to the next.
6.5 Cancer is a disease of uncontrolled cell division, and at root it is a disease of genes. It usually requires several mutations accumulating in one cell lineage over years. Two classes of genes are involved. Proto-oncogenes normally promote division in response to signals; mutations that make them permanently active turn them into oncogenes, like an accelerator stuck down. Tumour suppressor genes normally restrain division or trigger repair; mutations that inactivate them are like failed brakes.
6.6 The gene Ras, which relays growth signals, is a common oncogene, mutated in a large fraction of pancreatic, colorectal, and lung cancers. The gene p53, which halts the cell cycle or triggers cell death when DNA is damaged, is the most frequently mutated tumour suppressor in human cancers, and is sometimes called the guardian of the genome. Robert Weinberg, one of the teachers of MIT's 7.012, isolated one of the first human oncogenes, a mutant form of Ras, in 1982, and his laboratory identified the first tumour suppressor gene to be cloned, RB.
6.7 Because cancer arises from mutations, its risk rises with age and with exposure to mutagens such as tobacco smoke and ultraviolet light. Some people inherit one mutant copy of a tumour suppressor gene, such as BRCA1 or BRCA2, and so need fewer further mutations, which raises their risk. Modern treatment increasingly targets the specific molecular changes in a tumour, with drugs that block a particular mutant kinase, and with therapies that help the immune system recognise cancer cells.
6.8 The immune system defends the body against pathogens: bacteria, viruses, fungi, and parasites. Its first line is innate immunity, present from birth, including barriers such as skin, and cells such as macrophages and neutrophils that engulf invaders recognised by common molecular patterns. Innate responses are fast but general. They also cause inflammation, which brings more defenders to an infected site.
6.9 Adaptive immunity is slower to start but specific, and it remembers. Its main cells are lymphocytes. B cells make antibodies, Y-shaped proteins that bind to particular molecular shapes, called antigens, on a pathogen, marking it for destruction or blocking it. T cells come in two main kinds: helper T cells coordinate the response, and cytotoxic, or killer, T cells destroy infected cells. HIV causes AIDS by destroying helper T cells.
6.10 How can the body make antibodies against molecules it has never encountered? Each developing B cell shuffles gene segments to build a unique antibody, a process called V D J recombination, discovered by Susumu Tonegawa, who later worked at MIT. This generates many millions of different antibodies, a vast library. A pathogen's antigens select the few B cells whose antibodies fit, and those cells multiply, a process called clonal selection. Some remain as memory cells, so a second infection is met faster and more strongly.
6.11 Vaccination uses immune memory. A vaccine presents antigens from a pathogen, as a weakened or inactivated microbe, a purified protein, or, as described in lecture five, an mRNA that instructs cells to make the protein, without causing the disease. The immune system responds and forms memory cells. Edward Jenner's smallpox vaccine of 1796 began the practice, and a worldwide campaign led the World Health Organization to declare smallpox eradicated in 1980.
6.12 The theory that unifies all of biology is evolution. In 1859 Charles Darwin, in On the Origin of Species, argued that all living things descend with modification from common ancestors, and that the main mechanism is natural selection. Alfred Russel Wallace reached the same idea independently. The geneticist Theodosius Dobzhansky's famous phrase of 1973 sums it up: nothing in biology makes sense except in the light of evolution.
6.13 Natural selection follows from a few observable facts. Individuals in a population vary; much of that variation is heritable; more offspring are produced than can survive and reproduce; and some variants survive and reproduce better than others in a given environment. Over generations, the favoured heritable variants become more common. Selection does not plan ahead and does not produce perfection; it acts on whatever variation mutation and recombination supply, in the current environment.
6.14 Evolution is visible today. Bacteria evolve resistance to antibiotics, because any rare resistant cells survive treatment and multiply; overuse of antibiotics accelerates this. Insects evolve resistance to pesticides, and viruses such as influenza and SARS-CoV-2 evolve new variants that escape immunity. In Richard Lenski's long-term experiment, begun in 1988, populations of Escherichia coli have been followed for more than seventy-five thousand generations, and one population evolved the ability to use citrate, a nutrient its ancestors could not use in the presence of oxygen.
6.15 The molecular biology of this course is the strongest evidence for evolution. All life uses DNA, the same genetic code, ribosomes, and ATP, as expected from common descent. Comparing DNA sequences lets biologists reconstruct the tree of life and estimate when lineages diverged; humans and chimpanzees, for example, share about ninety-eight to ninety-nine per cent of their DNA in aligned regions. Engineers have even borrowed the logic of evolution in directed evolution, which breeds better enzymes in the laboratory, recognised with a share of the 2018 Nobel Prize in Chemistry awarded to Frances Arnold.
