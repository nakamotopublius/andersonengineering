[L1-title] Lecture 1. Atoms, Electrons, and the Periodic Table.

[1.1] Every atom has a tiny, dense nucleus containing protons and neutrons, surrounded by electrons. The proton carries one unit of positive charge, the electron one unit of negative charge, and the neutron none. The number of protons, called the atomic number Z, defines the element: hydrogen has one proton, carbon six, iron twenty-six. A neutral atom has as many electrons as protons. Atoms of the same element with different numbers of neutrons are called isotopes; carbon-12 and carbon-14 are both carbon.

[1.2] Atoms are small, and chemists count them in large batches. One mole is about six point zero two times ten to the twenty-three particles, a number called Avogadro's number. The molar mass of an element, in grams per mole, is numerically equal to its average atomic mass in atomic mass units. So twelve grams of carbon-12 contain one mole of atoms, and about eighteen grams of water contain one mole of water molecules. The mole lets an engineer move between the scale of a laboratory balance and the scale of atoms.

[1.3] Ernest Rutherford's experiments around 1911 showed that the positive charge and nearly all the mass of an atom sit in a nucleus about a hundred thousand times smaller than the atom itself. That discovery raised a puzzle. Classical physics predicts that an electron circling a nucleus should radiate energy and spiral inward within a tiny fraction of a second. Atoms are plainly stable, so classical physics had to be wrong at this scale.

[1.4] Part of the answer came from light. When hydrogen gas is excited by an electric discharge, it emits light only at certain sharp wavelengths, not a continuous rainbow. In 1913 Niels Bohr proposed that the electron in hydrogen can occupy only certain energy levels, with energies equal to minus thirteen point six electron volts divided by n squared, where n is a positive whole number. Light is emitted when the electron drops from a higher level to a lower one, and the photon carries away exactly the energy difference.

[1.5] The energy of a photon is Planck's constant h times its frequency, or equivalently h times the speed of light divided by its wavelength. So a fixed energy gap means a fixed wavelength, which explains the sharp lines. The drop from level three to level two in hydrogen releases about one point nine electron volts and gives red light near six hundred and fifty-six nanometres. The same idea, energy levels and photons, underlies lasers, light-emitting diodes, and the spectroscopy engineers use to identify materials.

[1.6] Bohr's model works for hydrogen but fails for atoms with more electrons. The modern picture comes from quantum mechanics, in which an electron is described by a wavefunction, and the square of the wavefunction gives the probability of finding the electron in a region of space. The allowed wavefunctions of an electron in an atom are called orbitals. An orbital is not a path; it is a three-dimensional cloud of probability.

[1.7] Each orbital is labelled by three quantum numbers. The principal quantum number n, equal to one, two, three and so on, sets the shell and mainly the energy and size. The angular momentum quantum number l runs from zero up to n minus one and sets the shape: l equals zero is an s orbital, which is spherical; one is p, shaped like a dumbbell; two is d; three is f. The magnetic quantum number m sub l runs from minus l to plus l and sets the orientation, so there are three p orbitals and five d orbitals in a shell.

[1.8] A fourth quantum number, the spin m sub s, can be plus one half or minus one half. The Pauli exclusion principle says that no two electrons in an atom can share all four quantum numbers, so each orbital holds at most two electrons, with opposite spins. That simple rule gives every shell its capacity: two electrons in the first shell, eight in the second, eighteen in the third, and in general two times n squared.

[1.9] To build the electron configuration of an atom, fill orbitals from the lowest energy upward, a rule called the aufbau principle. The usual order is 1s, 2s, 2p, 3s, 3p, 4s, 3d, 4p, and onward. Carbon, with six electrons, is 1s two, 2s two, 2p two. Sodium, with eleven, is 1s two, 2s two, 2p six, 3s one. Within a set of equal-energy orbitals, Hund's rule says electrons first occupy separate orbitals with parallel spins, so carbon's two 2p electrons sit in different p orbitals.

[1.10] The electrons in the outermost shell are called valence electrons, and they do nearly all of chemistry. The inner, or core, electrons are held tightly and rarely take part in bonding. Sodium has one valence electron, chlorine seven, and neon a full shell of eight. Atoms tend to gain, lose, or share electrons in ways that reach a filled valence shell, which is the idea behind the octet rule of thumb.

[1.11] The periodic table arranges the elements by atomic number into rows, called periods, and columns, called groups. Elements in the same group have the same arrangement of valence electrons, so they behave alike. The alkali metals in group one each have one s electron to lose; the halogens in group seventeen each need one electron to complete a shell; the noble gases in group eighteen already have full shells and are nearly inert. The table's shape, with its s, p, d, and f blocks, is the shape of the orbital filling order.

[1.12] Several properties change smoothly across the table, and they all follow from two effects: the number of shells, and the effective nuclear charge felt by the valence electrons. Effective nuclear charge is the charge of the nucleus minus the shielding by inner electrons. Across a period, protons are added while shielding grows only a little, so the valence electrons feel a stronger pull. Down a group, new shells are added, and the valence electrons sit farther out.

[1.13] Atomic radius therefore decreases across a period and increases down a group. Ionisation energy, the energy needed to remove the outermost electron from a gaseous atom, follows the opposite pattern: it rises across a period and falls down a group. Helium has the highest first ionisation energy of all the elements, about twenty-four point six electron volts, and caesium one of the lowest, under four electron volts. That is why the alkali metals react so readily: their single valence electron is easy to remove.

[1.14] Electronegativity measures how strongly an atom in a bond attracts the shared electrons. On the scale introduced by Linus Pauling, fluorine is the most electronegative element at about four, oxygen is about three point four, carbon about two point five, and sodium under one. Electronegativity rises across a period and falls down a group, like ionisation energy. The difference in electronegativity between two bonded atoms will tell us, in the next lecture, what kind of bond they form.

[1.15] Atoms can also become ions. Metals on the left of the table tend to lose electrons and form positive ions, called cations, such as sodium plus and magnesium two plus. Non-metals on the right tend to gain electrons and form negative ions, called anions, such as chloride minus and oxide two minus. A cation is smaller than its parent atom, because it has lost its outer shell or some of its electron repulsion; an anion is larger.

[1.16] To summarise: the atom is a nucleus with electrons in quantised orbitals, labelled by four quantum numbers and filled according to Pauli's principle and the aufbau order. The resulting valence configurations explain the periodic table and its trends in size, ionisation energy, and electronegativity. With that foundation, the next lecture asks how atoms join together.

[L2-title] Lecture 2. Chemical Bonding and Molecular Shape.

[2.1] Atoms bond because the bonded arrangement has lower energy than the separate atoms. When two atoms approach, their electrons are attracted to both nuclei, which lowers the energy, while the nuclei repel each other and the electrons repel each other, which raises it. The bond length is the distance at which the total energy is lowest, and the bond energy is the energy needed to pull the atoms apart again. Every type of bond is a different way of reaching that lower energy.

[2.2] An ionic bond forms when one atom transfers electrons to another, and the resulting ions attract each other electrostatically. Sodium gives up its 3s electron, chlorine accepts it, and sodium plus and chloride minus attract. In a solid like sodium chloride there are no separate molecules; each ion is surrounded by six ions of the opposite charge in a regular crystal. Ionic bonds form between atoms with a large difference in electronegativity, typically a metal and a non-metal.

[2.3] The strength of an ionic solid is measured by its lattice energy, the energy released when gaseous ions come together to form one mole of the crystal. For sodium chloride it is about seven hundred and eighty-seven kilojoules per mole. By Coulomb's law, the attraction grows with the product of the ionic charges and falls with the distance between them. So magnesium oxide, with charges of two plus and two minus and smaller ions, has a lattice energy nearly five times larger and melts at a far higher temperature, near two thousand eight hundred degrees Celsius.

[2.4] A covalent bond forms when two atoms share a pair of electrons. Two hydrogen atoms share their electrons to make H two; two chlorine atoms share one pair to make Cl two. Atoms can share two pairs, a double bond, as in oxygen, O two, or three pairs, a triple bond, as in nitrogen, N two. More shared pairs mean a shorter and stronger bond. The triple bond in nitrogen is so strong, about nine hundred and forty-five kilojoules per mole, that nitrogen gas is very unreactive.

[2.5] Lewis structures are a bookkeeping method for covalent bonding. Count the valence electrons of all the atoms, connect the atoms with single bonds, then place the remaining electrons as lone pairs so that each atom other than hydrogen reaches eight electrons where possible. In water, oxygen has two bonds to hydrogen and two lone pairs. In carbon dioxide, carbon forms a double bond to each oxygen. Formal charges help choose between possible structures, and when several equally good structures exist, the real molecule is an average of them, a situation called resonance.

[2.6] Most bonds are neither purely ionic nor purely covalent. When two different atoms share electrons, the more electronegative atom pulls the pair toward itself, creating a polar covalent bond with a partial negative charge on one end and a partial positive charge on the other. A rough guide is that an electronegativity difference below about zero point four gives a nearly non-polar bond, a difference above about one point seven gives a mostly ionic bond, and values between give polar covalent bonds.

[2.7] The shape of a molecule is predicted well by valence shell electron pair repulsion, or VESPER. Electron domains around a central atom, which are bonds and lone pairs, arrange themselves as far apart as possible. Two domains give a linear shape, as in carbon dioxide, with a bond angle of one hundred and eighty degrees. Three give a flat triangle at one hundred and twenty degrees. Four give a tetrahedron, with angles of about one hundred and nine point five degrees, as in methane.

[2.8] Lone pairs count as domains but take up more room than bonding pairs. Ammonia has four domains, three bonds and one lone pair, so its atoms form a trigonal pyramid with angles of about one hundred and seven degrees. Water has two bonds and two lone pairs, so it is bent, with an angle of about one hundred and four point five degrees. These small differences have large consequences: the bent shape of water is why water is polar.

[2.9] A molecule can have polar bonds and still be non-polar overall, if its shape makes the bond polarities cancel. Carbon dioxide has two polar carbon-oxygen bonds, but they point in opposite directions, so the molecule has no net dipole. Water's two polar bonds point at an angle, so their effects add, and water has a substantial dipole moment. Polarity governs solubility, boiling point, and how molecules respond to electric fields, which is how a microwave oven heats food.

[2.10] Valence bond theory describes bonds as overlaps of atomic orbitals, and it explains the tetrahedral angle of methane through hybridisation. Carbon mixes its one 2s and three 2p orbitals into four equivalent sp three hybrid orbitals pointing to the corners of a tetrahedron. In ethylene, carbon uses sp two hybrids for a flat framework and a leftover p orbital for a second bond. A bond formed by head-on overlap is a sigma bond; a bond formed by side-by-side overlap of p orbitals is a pi bond. A double bond is one sigma and one pi.

[2.11] Molecular orbital theory goes further. When atoms combine, their atomic orbitals combine into molecular orbitals that spread over the whole molecule. Some are bonding orbitals, lower in energy than the originals, and some are antibonding, higher in energy. The bond order is half the difference between the number of bonding and antibonding electrons. Molecular orbital theory correctly predicts that the oxygen molecule has two unpaired electrons, which is why liquid oxygen is attracted to a magnet, something simple Lewis structures miss.

[2.12] Metals bond in yet another way. In a metal, the valence electrons are not tied to particular atoms; they are shared by the whole solid, forming what is often called a sea of electrons around a lattice of positive ions. This delocalised bonding explains why metals conduct electricity and heat so well, why they reflect light and look shiny, and why they can be bent and drawn into wire: layers of atoms can slide without breaking particular bonds.

[2.13] Between molecules act weaker forces called intermolecular forces. London dispersion forces arise from momentary fluctuations in electron clouds and act between all molecules; they grow with molecular size. Dipole-dipole forces act between polar molecules. Hydrogen bonds, the strongest of the three, form when hydrogen bonded to nitrogen, oxygen, or fluorine is attracted to a lone pair on a nearby nitrogen, oxygen, or fluorine atom.

[2.14] Hydrogen bonding explains much of the behaviour of water. Water, with a molar mass of only eighteen grams per mole, boils at one hundred degrees Celsius, while hydrogen sulphide, which is heavier, boils at about minus sixty. Hydrogen bonds also make ice less dense than liquid water, because the open hydrogen-bonded lattice of ice holds molecules farther apart. That is why ice floats and why pipes can burst when water freezes in them.

[2.15] For an engineer, the type of bonding predicts a great deal before any measurement is made. Ionic solids tend to be hard, brittle, and high-melting, and they conduct electricity only when molten or dissolved. Covalent network solids such as diamond and quartz are very hard and very high-melting. Molecular solids held by intermolecular forces, such as wax and ice, are soft and melt at low temperatures. Metals are ductile conductors.

[2.16] To summarise: bonds form because they lower energy. Electron transfer gives ionic bonds, electron sharing gives covalent bonds, and delocalised electrons give metallic bonds, with polar bonds in between. VESPER predicts molecular shape, shape determines polarity, and intermolecular forces determine how molecules hold together in liquids and solids. Next we look at the ordered arrangement of atoms in crystals.

[L3-title] Lecture 3. Crystals and the Solid State.

[3.1] Most engineering solids are crystalline: their atoms sit in a regular pattern that repeats in three dimensions over very many atomic spacings. Others are amorphous, with atoms in a disordered arrangement like a frozen liquid; window glass is the everyday example. The difference matters. Crystals melt at a sharp temperature and break along definite planes, while glasses soften gradually over a range of temperatures. Solid-state chemistry, the backbone of MIT's three oh nine one, studies how atomic arrangement produces these properties.

[3.2] A crystal is described by a lattice and a basis. The lattice is an infinite array of points, each with identical surroundings. The basis is the atom or group of atoms attached to every lattice point. The smallest repeating box from which the whole crystal can be built by stacking is the unit cell. In 1848 Auguste Bravais showed that there are only fourteen distinct types of lattice in three dimensions, now called the Bravais lattices, grouped into seven crystal systems from cubic to triclinic.

[3.3] The cubic lattices are the most important for metals. In simple cubic, there is one lattice point at each corner of a cube. In body-centred cubic, abbreviated B C C, there is an extra point at the centre of the cube. In face-centred cubic, abbreviated F C C, there is an extra point at the centre of each face. Iron at room temperature is B C C; aluminium, copper, silver, and gold are F C C. Polonium is the only element that crystallises in simple cubic form.

[3.4] To count atoms in a unit cell, remember that a corner atom is shared by eight cells, a face atom by two, and a body-centre atom by none. A simple cubic cell therefore contains one atom: eight corners times one eighth. A B C C cell contains two: one from the corners plus one at the centre. An F C C cell contains four: one from the corners plus six faces times one half. These counts, together with the size of the cell, give the density of a crystal from atomic data alone.

[3.5] Packing efficiency is the fraction of the cell's volume filled by atoms modelled as hard spheres. Simple cubic fills only about fifty-two per cent of space. B C C fills about sixty-eight per cent. F C C fills about seventy-four per cent, the largest value possible for equal spheres, the same as in a well-stacked pile of oranges. The hexagonal close-packed structure, found in magnesium, titanium, and zinc, also reaches seventy-four per cent; it differs from F C C only in the order in which close-packed layers are stacked.

[3.6] Each atom's number of nearest neighbours is called its coordination number. It is six in simple cubic, eight in B C C, and twelve in both F C C and hexagonal close packing. Close-packed F C C metals have many planes along which layers can slip, which is why copper, aluminium, and gold are so ductile. Ionic crystals follow the same geometry with two kinds of ions; in sodium chloride the sodium and chloride ions each form an F C C lattice, interpenetrating, and each ion has six neighbours of the opposite charge.

[3.7] Directions and planes in a crystal are named with Miller indices. To find the indices of a plane, take its intercepts with the three axes in units of the cell edge, take the reciprocals, and clear fractions to the smallest whole numbers. A plane that cuts the x axis at one and runs parallel to the other two axes is the one-zero-zero plane. A plane cutting all three axes at one is the one-one-one plane. Engineers use these labels to describe slip, cleavage, and the orientation of silicon wafers.

[3.8] How do we know any of this? The spacing between atomic planes is a fraction of a nanometre, comparable to the wavelength of X-rays. When X-rays strike a crystal, each plane of atoms scatters a little of the beam. In 1913 William Henry Bragg and his son William Lawrence Bragg showed that the scattered waves reinforce one another only at particular angles, so a crystal produces a pattern of sharp spots or peaks.

[3.9] Bragg's law states the condition: n times the wavelength equals two times the plane spacing d times the sine of the angle theta between the beam and the planes, where n is a whole number. The extra distance travelled by the ray reflected from the lower of two planes is two d sine theta, and the waves add when that extra distance is a whole number of wavelengths. Measure the angles of strong reflections with X-rays of known wavelength, and you can calculate the spacings and identify the structure.

[3.10] X-ray diffraction is one of the most widely used tools in engineering and science. It identifies minerals and corrosion products, measures residual stress in welded parts, checks the phase of steels and ceramics, and, in the form of crystallography, revealed the double helix of DNA through the work of Rosalind Franklin, Maurice Wilkins, James Watson, and Francis Crick. In three oh nine one, students see diffraction patterns as direct evidence that atoms are arranged in lattices.

[3.11] Real crystals are never perfect, and their imperfections, called defects, control many properties. Point defects involve single sites: a vacancy is a missing atom, an interstitial is an extra atom squeezed between regular sites, and a substitutional impurity is a foreign atom on a regular site. The number of vacancies grows exponentially with temperature, which is why atoms move through solids much faster when they are hot.

[3.12] Line defects are called dislocations: an extra half-plane of atoms inserted partway into a crystal, or a related distortion. Metals deform permanently by the movement of dislocations, which can glide through a crystal by breaking and remaking only a row of bonds at a time. That is why the measured strength of ordinary metals is far below the strength calculated for a perfect crystal. Engineers strengthen metals by obstructing dislocations, through alloying, cold working, and grain refinement.

[3.13] Planar defects include grain boundaries. Most metal parts are polycrystalline, made of many small crystals, called grains, with different orientations, meeting at boundaries. Smaller grains mean more boundaries, more obstacles to dislocations, and usually a stronger, tougher metal. Heat treatment and controlled cooling let a metallurgist choose the grain size, which is one reason the same steel can be made soft for forming or hard for cutting.

[3.14] Defects are not always faults. Adding a small amount of carbon to iron, as interstitial atoms, makes steel. Adding a few parts per million of phosphorus or boron to silicon, as substitutional impurities, makes the semiconductors in every chip. Colour centres in gemstones, ionic conduction in solid electrolytes, and the hardening of aluminium alloys used in aircraft all depend on deliberately chosen defects.

[3.15] Amorphous solids deserve a final word. When a liquid is cooled quickly, or when its molecules are too awkward to arrange easily, it can become rigid without crystallising. Silica glass, with its network of silicon-oxygen tetrahedra joined at random angles, is the classic example. Glasses have no long-range order but do have short-range order: each silicon still has four oxygen neighbours. Many polymers are partly amorphous and partly crystalline, and the balance sets their stiffness and clarity.

[3.16] To summarise: crystalline solids are lattices with a basis, built from unit cells. Cubic metals pack in simple, body-centred, or face-centred arrangements with different packing efficiencies and coordination numbers. X-ray diffraction and Bragg's law reveal the structure, and point, line, and planar defects control strength, conductivity, and much else. Next we ask which way chemical changes go, and how far.

[L4-title] Lecture 4. Thermodynamics and Chemical Equilibrium.

[4.1] Chemical thermodynamics answers two questions: how much energy does a reaction release or absorb, and will it go by itself? It does not say how fast a reaction will go; that is the subject of kinetics, in lecture six. Engineers use thermodynamics to design furnaces and fuel systems, to choose processes for extracting metals from ores, and to predict whether a material will corrode in a given environment.

[4.2] The first law of thermodynamics says that energy is conserved: the change in the internal energy of a system equals the heat added to it plus the work done on it. Most chemistry happens at constant pressure, open to the atmosphere, so chemists use a quantity called enthalpy, written H. The change in enthalpy, delta H, equals the heat absorbed by a reaction at constant pressure. A negative delta H means heat is released, an exothermic reaction; a positive delta H means heat is absorbed, an endothermic reaction.

[4.3] Burning methane is strongly exothermic, releasing about eight hundred and ninety kilojoules per mole of methane when the water formed is liquid. Dissolving ammonium nitrate in water is endothermic, which is how instant cold packs work. Because enthalpy is a state function, depending only on the starting and ending states, enthalpy changes can be added. Hess's law says the enthalpy change of an overall reaction is the sum of the changes for any series of steps that add up to it.

[4.4] Tabulated standard enthalpies of formation make this practical. The standard enthalpy of formation of a compound is the enthalpy change when one mole of it forms from its elements in their standard states, which is zero for the elements themselves. The enthalpy change of any reaction equals the sum of the enthalpies of formation of the products minus the sum for the reactants, each multiplied by its coefficient in the balanced equation. Bond energies give rougher estimates by the same logic: bonds broken cost energy, bonds formed release it.

[4.5] Releasing heat is not enough to decide whether a change happens. Ice melts spontaneously at room temperature, which absorbs heat. The missing idea is entropy, written S, a measure of the number of microscopic arrangements consistent with the state of a system. Ludwig Boltzmann expressed it as S equals k log W, where k is Boltzmann's constant and W is the number of arrangements. Gases have more entropy than liquids, and liquids more than solids; mixing and dissolving usually increase entropy.

[4.6] The second law of thermodynamics says that in any spontaneous process the total entropy of the system plus its surroundings increases. Heat released into the surroundings raises their entropy by the heat divided by the absolute temperature. Combining the system's entropy change with this effect on the surroundings gives a single quantity that depends on the system alone, called the Gibbs free energy, after the American scientist Josiah Willard Gibbs.

[4.7] The change in Gibbs free energy is delta G equals delta H minus T times delta S, where T is the absolute temperature in kelvin. At constant temperature and pressure, a process with negative delta G is spontaneous, one with positive delta G is not, and delta G equal to zero means the system is at equilibrium. Spontaneous here means thermodynamically favoured, not fast: diamond turning into graphite has a negative delta G at room temperature, but it is immeasurably slow.

[4.8] The equation shows how temperature tips the balance. If delta H is negative and delta S positive, the process is spontaneous at every temperature. If delta H is positive and delta S negative, it is never spontaneous. When both have the same sign, temperature decides. Melting ice has positive delta H and positive delta S, so it becomes spontaneous above the temperature at which T delta S exceeds delta H, which is zero degrees Celsius, or two hundred and seventy-three kelvin, at one atmosphere.

[4.9] Many reactions do not go to completion; they reach a state of chemical equilibrium in which the forward and reverse reactions proceed at equal rates and the amounts of reactants and products stop changing. Equilibrium is dynamic: molecules keep reacting in both directions. For a reaction a A plus b B going to c C plus d D, the equilibrium constant K equals the concentration of C to the power c times the concentration of D to the power d, divided by the concentration of A to the power a times that of B to the power b, all measured at equilibrium.

[4.10] A large K, much greater than one, means products dominate at equilibrium; a small K, much less than one, means reactants dominate. For gases the constant is usually written with partial pressures. Pure solids and pure liquids do not appear in the expression. Away from equilibrium, the same expression evaluated with current concentrations is called the reaction quotient Q. If Q is less than K the reaction moves forward, and if Q is greater than K it moves in reverse, until Q equals K.

[4.11] The link between free energy and equilibrium is one of the most useful equations in chemistry: the standard free energy change, delta G nought, equals minus R T times the natural logarithm of K, where R is the gas constant, eight point three one four joules per mole per kelvin. A negative delta G nought means K is greater than one. Because of the logarithm, every change of about five point seven kilojoules per mole in delta G nought changes K by a factor of ten at room temperature.

[4.12] More generally, delta G equals delta G nought plus R T times the natural logarithm of Q. This tells us the driving force under any conditions, not only standard ones. As a reaction proceeds, Q approaches K and delta G approaches zero; the reaction runs downhill in free energy until there is no slope left. The same equation will reappear in lecture five, where it connects to the voltage of a battery.

[4.13] Le Chatelier's principle predicts how an equilibrium responds to a disturbance: the system shifts in the direction that partly counteracts the change. Add a reactant and more product forms. Remove a product and the reaction moves forward to replace it. Increase the pressure on a gas-phase equilibrium and it shifts toward the side with fewer moles of gas. Raise the temperature and an exothermic reaction shifts back toward reactants, because K itself becomes smaller.

[4.14] The Haber-Bosch synthesis of ammonia from nitrogen and hydrogen is the classic engineering example. The reaction is exothermic and reduces four moles of gas to two. Le Chatelier suggests low temperature and high pressure for a high yield, but at low temperature the reaction is far too slow. Industrial plants compromise, at roughly four hundred to five hundred degrees Celsius and pressures of one hundred to three hundred atmospheres, with an iron catalyst and continuous removal of ammonia. Its fertiliser feeds a large share of the world's population.

[4.15] Thermodynamics also guides materials processing. An Ellingham diagram plots delta G nought for the formation of metal oxides against temperature. It shows, for example, that carbon becomes a better reducing agent at high temperature, because the reaction of carbon with oxygen to form carbon monoxide has a free energy that becomes more negative as temperature rises. That is the thermodynamic reason a blast furnace can reduce iron ore with coke.

[4.16] To summarise: enthalpy measures heat at constant pressure, entropy measures the number of arrangements, and the Gibbs free energy, delta G equals delta H minus T delta S, decides whether a change is favoured. The equilibrium constant tells how far a reaction goes, delta G nought equals minus R T ln K connects the two, and Le Chatelier's principle predicts the response to disturbances. Next we apply these ideas to acids, bases, and electrochemistry.

[L5-title] Lecture 5. Acids, Bases, and Electrochemistry.

[5.1] Acids and bases are everywhere in engineering: in water treatment, concrete chemistry, battery electrolytes, food processing, and the corrosion of pipes. In the Brønsted-Lowry definition, an acid is a proton donor and a base is a proton acceptor, where a proton means a hydrogen ion. When hydrogen chloride dissolves in water it donates a proton to a water molecule, forming hydronium, H three O plus, and chloride. The broader Lewis definition calls a base an electron-pair donor and an acid an electron-pair acceptor.

[5.2] Water itself is both a weak acid and a weak base. A tiny fraction of water molecules transfer protons to one another, forming hydronium and hydroxide ions. At twenty-five degrees Celsius the product of their concentrations, called K w, is one times ten to the minus fourteen. In pure water the two concentrations are equal, each one times ten to the minus seven moles per litre. This water equilibrium links the acidity and basicity of every aqueous solution.

[5.3] Because these concentrations span many powers of ten, chemists use a logarithmic scale. The pH is minus the base-ten logarithm of the hydronium concentration in moles per litre. Pure water at twenty-five degrees has pH seven, which is neutral. Acidic solutions have pH below seven and basic solutions above seven. Each unit of pH is a factor of ten, so a solution of pH three has a hundred times more hydronium than one of pH five. Similarly, pOH is minus the logarithm of the hydroxide concentration, and pH plus pOH equals fourteen at twenty-five degrees.

[5.4] A strong acid such as hydrochloric, nitric, or sulphuric acid in its first proton dissociates essentially completely in water. A weak acid, such as acetic acid in vinegar, dissociates only partly, and its equilibrium constant, the acid dissociation constant K a, measures the extent. The smaller K a, or the larger its negative logarithm p K a, the weaker the acid. Acetic acid has a p K a of about four point seven six. Strong bases include sodium hydroxide; ammonia is a typical weak base.

[5.5] A buffer is a solution that resists changes in pH when small amounts of acid or base are added. It contains a weak acid together with its conjugate base, such as acetic acid and acetate, in comparable amounts. Added acid is consumed by the base, and added base is consumed by the acid. The Henderson-Hasselbalch equation gives the pH: pH equals p K a plus the logarithm of the ratio of conjugate base to acid. A buffer works best within about one pH unit of its p K a. Human blood is buffered near pH seven point four.

[5.6] In a titration, a base of known concentration is added to an acid until the acid is just consumed, the equivalence point, which is often detected by an indicator dye or a pH meter. For a strong acid with a strong base, the equivalence point is at pH seven. For a weak acid with a strong base, it is above seven, because the conjugate base of the weak acid is itself slightly basic. Halfway to the equivalence point of a weak acid, the pH equals the p K a, a fact used to measure p K a values.

[5.7] The second great class of reactions involves the transfer of electrons rather than protons. Oxidation is the loss of electrons, and reduction is the gain of electrons; the two always occur together in a redox reaction. A memory aid is OIL RIG: oxidation is loss, reduction is gain. When zinc metal is placed in a solution of copper sulphate, zinc atoms are oxidised to zinc ions and copper ions are reduced to copper metal, which coats the zinc.

[5.8] Oxidation numbers keep track of electrons. An element on its own has oxidation number zero; a simple ion has its charge; oxygen in compounds is usually minus two and hydrogen usually plus one; and the oxidation numbers in a neutral compound add to zero. In iron oxide, Fe two O three, each iron is plus three. A species whose oxidation number rises is oxidised and is called the reducing agent; one whose oxidation number falls is reduced and is called the oxidising agent.

[5.9] If the two halves of a redox reaction are separated, the electrons must travel through an external wire, and the reaction can do electrical work. That arrangement is a galvanic, or voltaic, cell. In the Daniell cell, a zinc electrode dips into zinc sulphate solution and a copper electrode into copper sulphate, with a salt bridge to let ions move between them. Oxidation happens at the anode, here zinc, and reduction at the cathode, here copper. Electrons flow through the wire from anode to cathode.

[5.10] Each half-reaction has a standard reduction potential, measured in volts against the standard hydrogen electrode, which is assigned zero. Copper two plus going to copper has a standard potential of about plus zero point three four volts, and zinc two plus going to zinc about minus zero point seven six volts. The standard cell voltage is the cathode potential minus the anode potential: zero point three four minus minus zero point seven six, or one point one zero volts for the Daniell cell. A positive cell voltage means the reaction is spontaneous as written.

[5.11] Cell voltage and free energy are directly linked: delta G nought equals minus n F E nought, where n is the number of moles of electrons transferred, F is the Faraday constant, about ninety-six thousand four hundred and eighty-five coulombs per mole, and E nought is the standard cell voltage. For the Daniell cell, with two electrons, delta G nought is about minus two hundred and twelve kilojoules per mole. A positive voltage corresponds to a negative free energy change, so lecture four's criterion for spontaneity reappears as a voltage.

[5.12] Under non-standard conditions the voltage is given by the Nernst equation, named after Walther Nernst: E equals E nought minus R T over n F times the natural logarithm of Q. At twenty-five degrees Celsius this is E equals E nought minus zero point zero five nine two divided by n, times the base-ten logarithm of Q. As a battery discharges, products accumulate, Q rises, and the voltage falls. When Q reaches K, the voltage is zero and the battery is flat: the cell has reached equilibrium.

[5.13] Batteries are galvanic cells engineered for energy density, power, safety, and life. An alkaline cell uses zinc and manganese dioxide. A lead-acid car battery uses lead, lead dioxide, and sulphuric acid, and gives about two volts per cell, so six cells give twelve volts. A lithium-ion battery shuttles lithium ions between a graphite anode and a metal oxide cathode, giving about three point six to three point seven volts per cell, which is why it dominates phones and electric vehicles.

[5.14] Running a cell in reverse, by forcing current through it with an external power supply, is electrolysis. It drives non-spontaneous reactions: recharging a battery, electroplating, producing chlorine and sodium hydroxide from brine, and refining copper. Aluminium is produced by electrolysis of alumina dissolved in molten cryolite in the Hall-Héroult process. Faraday's laws relate the amount of chemical change to the charge passed: one mole of electrons is one faraday of charge.

[5.15] Corrosion is unwanted electrochemistry. When iron rusts, small regions of the metal act as anodes where iron is oxidised, and other regions act as cathodes where oxygen dissolved in water is reduced. Water and oxygen are both needed, and salt speeds the process by making the water a better conductor. Engineers fight corrosion with coatings and paint, with galvanising, which covers steel with zinc that corrodes first, with sacrificial anodes of zinc or magnesium on ships and pipelines, and with alloys such as stainless steel that form a protective oxide film.

[5.16] To summarise: acids donate protons and bases accept them; pH measures acidity on a logarithmic scale tied to the water equilibrium; buffers resist pH change. Redox reactions transfer electrons, galvanic cells turn them into electrical work, standard potentials give cell voltages, delta G nought equals minus n F E nought, and the Nernst equation handles real conditions. Batteries, electrolysis, and corrosion are all the same chemistry. Next we ask how fast reactions go.

[L6-title] Lecture 6. Reaction Rates, Diffusion, and Engineering Materials.

[6.1] Thermodynamics tells us whether a reaction can go; kinetics tells us how fast it goes. The rate of a reaction is the change in concentration of a reactant or product per unit time. A rate law expresses how the rate depends on concentrations, typically as a rate constant k times each concentration raised to some power. The powers are called the orders of the reaction with respect to each species, and they must be found by experiment, not read from the balanced equation.

[6.2] In a first-order reaction the rate is proportional to the concentration of one reactant. The concentration then decays exponentially: it equals the starting concentration times e to the minus k t. The half-life, the time for the concentration to fall by half, is the natural logarithm of two divided by k, about zero point six nine three over k, and it does not depend on how much you start with. Radioactive decay is first order, which is why carbon-14, with a half-life of about five thousand seven hundred years, can date ancient materials.

[6.3] In a second-order reaction with a single reactant, the rate is proportional to the square of its concentration, and one over the concentration grows linearly with time. Its half-life is not constant; it gets longer as the reaction proceeds. In a zero-order reaction the rate does not depend on concentration at all, which happens for example when a reaction occurs on a catalyst surface that is completely covered. Plotting data in these different ways, concentration, log of concentration, and one over concentration, against time, identifies the order.

[6.4] Why do reactions need time at all, even when they release energy? Most reactions must pass through a high-energy arrangement, called the transition state or activated complex, on the way from reactants to products. The energy needed to reach it is the activation energy. Only collisions with enough energy, and the right orientation, lead to reaction. A pile of wood in air is thermodynamically unstable, but it does not burst into flame until something supplies the activation energy.

[6.5] The Arrhenius equation, proposed by Svante Arrhenius in 1889, expresses the temperature dependence: the rate constant k equals A times e to the minus E a over R T, where A is a frequency factor, E a the activation energy, R the gas constant, and T the absolute temperature. A plot of the natural log of k against one over T is a straight line with slope minus E a over R. Because of the exponential, rates are very sensitive to temperature; for many reactions near room temperature a rise of ten degrees roughly doubles the rate.

[6.6] A catalyst speeds a reaction by providing a different pathway with a lower activation energy, and it is not consumed. A catalyst does not change delta G or the equilibrium constant; it speeds the forward and reverse reactions alike, so equilibrium is reached sooner. The platinum, palladium, and rhodium in a car's catalytic converter oxidise carbon monoxide and unburned fuel and reduce nitrogen oxides. Enzymes, the catalysts of living cells, are the subject of the next course, Engineering 506 B.

[6.7] In solids, the rate of many processes is limited by how fast atoms can move. Atoms in a crystal jump from site to neighbouring site, most often into vacancies. This random hopping produces a net flow from regions of high concentration to regions of low concentration, called diffusion. Diffusion controls the hardening of steel surfaces, the doping of silicon chips, the sintering of ceramic powders, and the slow failure of many materials at high temperature.

[6.8] Fick's first law says that the diffusion flux, the amount crossing unit area per unit time, equals minus the diffusion coefficient D times the concentration gradient. The minus sign means atoms flow down the gradient. The diffusion coefficient itself follows an Arrhenius law, D equals D nought times e to the minus Q over R T, where Q is the activation energy for diffusion. That is why carburising, which diffuses carbon into the surface of steel gears, is done at around nine hundred degrees Celsius rather than at room temperature.

[6.9] Fick's second law describes how concentration profiles change with time. A useful consequence is that the typical distance atoms diffuse grows with the square root of D times t. To diffuse twice as deep takes four times as long. Engineers use this square-root rule constantly: to estimate how long a heat treatment must last, how deep a dopant will penetrate in a furnace step, or how long a protective coating will hold back a corrosive species.

[6.10] We can now tour the main classes of engineering materials with the chemistry of this course in hand. Metals are held by metallic bonding, usually in B C C, F C C, or hexagonal close-packed structures. Their ductility comes from dislocation motion, and their strength is tuned by alloying, grain size, and heat treatment. Steel, an alloy of iron with a small amount of carbon, is still the most used structural metal in the world.

[6.11] Ceramics are compounds of metals with non-metals, such as alumina, silicon carbide, and the silicates in brick, cement, and porcelain. Their ionic and covalent bonds make them hard, stiff, heat-resistant, and chemically stable, but brittle, because dislocations hardly move and cracks spread easily from small flaws. Glasses are amorphous ceramics. Portland cement sets not by drying but by hydration reactions that form calcium silicate hydrate, which binds the sand and stone in concrete.

[6.12] Polymers are long chain molecules built by joining many small units called monomers. Polyethylene is a chain of thousands of CH two units joined by covalent carbon-carbon bonds. Within a chain the bonds are strong; between chains act much weaker intermolecular forces. Thermoplastics such as polyethylene and nylon soften on heating and can be remoulded; thermosets such as epoxy form covalent cross-links between chains and do not melt. Above its glass transition temperature an amorphous polymer turns from glassy to rubbery.

[6.13] Semiconductors connect chemistry to electronics. In a solid, the energy levels of many atoms merge into bands. In a metal the highest occupied band is partly filled, so electrons move freely. In an insulator a wide band gap separates the filled valence band from the empty conduction band. A semiconductor has a modest gap; silicon's is about one point one electron volts, small enough that some electrons are thermally excited across it, and the conductivity rises with temperature.

[6.14] Doping controls a semiconductor's conductivity precisely. Silicon has four valence electrons. Replacing a few silicon atoms with phosphorus, which has five, supplies extra electrons and makes n-type silicon. Replacing them with boron, which has three, creates missing electrons called holes and makes p-type silicon. Joining p-type and n-type regions makes a p-n junction, the basis of diodes, solar cells, light-emitting diodes, and transistors. The defects of lecture three and the diffusion of this lecture are how chips are made.

[6.15] Looking back over the course, the same few ideas explain all of these materials. Electron configurations from lecture one decide which bonds form. Bonding from lecture two decides whether a solid is a metal, ceramic, polymer, or semiconductor. Structure and defects from lecture three set strength and conductivity. Thermodynamics from lecture four and electrochemistry from lecture five decide what is stable and what corrodes, and kinetics and diffusion from this lecture decide how fast anything changes.
